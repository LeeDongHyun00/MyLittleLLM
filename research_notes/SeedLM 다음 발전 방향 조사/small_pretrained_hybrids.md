# Small pretrained code models (≤3B, 4-bit, CPU-only) combined with a symbolic rule engine and tiny from-scratch models

*Conventions. Numbers are pass@1 in % unless stated. HE/HE+ = HumanEval/HumanEval+, M/M+ = MBPP/MBPP+ (EvalPlus), LCB = LiveCodeBench, BCB = BigCodeBench. "[>3B]" marks a result for a model above the 3B limit. "UNVERIFIED" marks an item I could not confirm from a primary source. Research date: 2026-11-23. The public EvalPlus leaderboard data file (125 models) contains no Qwen2.5-Coder ≤14B, Qwen3, Gemma 3/4, SmolLM or Phi-4 rows, so most recent small-model numbers below come from vendor technical reports that use the EvalPlus harness.*

## 1. Landscape (as of Nov 2026) of small open models usable for code on a 4-core CPU with 4-bit quantization

### Takeaway
On MBPP+ the best verified ≤3B scores are still Qwen2.5-Coder-3B-Instruct 62.4 (research-only licence), OpenCoder-1.5B-Instruct 61.9, rStar-Coder-1.5B 60.8 and Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct 59.4 (Apache-2.0); going from 1.5B to 3B buys only +3.0 MBPP+ points for about 1.9x the bytes per token, while 7B is the first size with a large jump (71.7). The 2026 releases (Qwen3.5 small, Gemma 4 E2B/E4B, MiniCPM5-2B) report LiveCodeBench in thinking mode rather than HumanEval/MBPP, and thinking mode costs thousands of output tokens per answer, which is a poor fit for a CPU budget of roughly 10-20 tokens/s at 1.5B.

### Inferences
- Speed estimate for a 4-core x86 VM. The 7B Xeon measurement (3.8 tok/s for a roughly 4.7 GB file) and the cloud-Xeon 1.5B measurement (17 tok/s for about 1.1 GB) both imply about 18 GB/s of effective bandwidth. With the official file sizes that gives roughly 35 tok/s for Qwen2.5-Coder-0.5B, 16 tok/s for 1.5B and 8-9 tok/s for 3B at Q4_K_M with 4 threads; a Pi-5-class machine gets about half of that. These are estimates, not measurements, and should be replaced by `llama-bench` numbers from the actual machine.
- At equal CPU time, two samples from the 1.5B model cost about the same as one from the 3B model, and the independent study's pass@2 of the 1.5B model (0.60) matches the 3B model's pass@1 (0.59). Since the pipeline already has an execution-based selector, "1.5B with more samples" is at least as good a use of CPU seconds as "3B once" on MBPP-style tasks, and it avoids the 3B research-only licence.
- The measured 1.5B pipeline of this project (102/151 = 67.5% on its MBPP+ test split) is already above the official single-shot MBPP+ of the 3B instruct model (62.4) and 4 points under the 7B model (71.7); the splits are not identical, so this is only a rough placement.
- Candidates worth a head-to-head run as a second, differently trained voter: OpenCoder-1.5B-Instruct (MBPP+ 61.9, custom licence), Qwen3-1.7B without thinking (base MBPP+ 59.25 without any code-specific tuning), Qwen3.5-2B and Gemma 4 E2B (no MBPP+ published; E2B's 5.1B total parameters make it slower than its "2B" name suggests).
- Thinking-mode models (Qwen3 with thinking, MiniCPM5-2B, rStar-Coder-1.5B) are a poor fit here: rStar-Coder-1.5B moves MBPP+ by only +1.5 while LCB moves +33.6, and a 3-5K-token answer takes 3-8 minutes at 10-16 tok/s.

### Gaps
- No HumanEval+/MBPP+ numbers found for Gemma 3 270M, Gemma 3 1B/4B, Gemma 4, Qwen3.5 small, SmolLM2/SmolLM3 instruct, Granite 4.x nano, LFM2/LFM2.5 or MiniCPM5 from a primary source; none of these are on the EvalPlus leaderboard.
- No primary measurement of Qwen2.5-Coder 0.5B/1.5B/3B tokens/s on a 4-core x86 CPU was found; all x86 figures are for other models or come from informal posts.
- No published evaluation of how 4-bit quantization changes MBPP+ for these exact models was found (the project's own 0.5B result of 56/151 is the only data point).
- Release dates of Qwen3.5 small (reported as March 2026) and Gemma 4 (reported as April 2026) come from search summaries of news pages, not from the model cards.

## 2. Inference-time techniques with execution feedback: measured gains for small models and token cost

### Takeaway
For small models the gains come from sampling plus execution-based selection, and most of that gain depends on a trustworthy filter (the visible example) and on comparing candidates' behaviour on extra inputs; repair loops and multi-agent flows add little at this size and cost 10-100 model calls. Small models write valid test inputs but unreliable expected outputs (for a 7B coder, under 45% of self-written test suites are fully correct), so they should supply inputs, not oracles.

### Cited Findings

**Sampling and coverage**
- Repeated sampling raises coverage (any sample correct) log-linearly over four orders of magnitude; Gemma-2B on CodeContests goes from 0.02% with one sample to 7.1% with 10,000. Selection is the bottleneck without a verifier: Llama-3-8B on MATH has coverage 82.9% at 100 samples and 98.44% at 10,000, but majority voting or a reward model only moves 40.50% to 41.41%. At a fixed FLOP budget Llama-3-8B beats 70B on MiniF2F, GSM8K and MATH, but on CodeContests the 70B model is almost always more cost-effective [>3B]. — [Brown et al. 2024, Large Language Monkeys](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787)
- PlanSearch reports pass@200 (oracle over 200 candidates), not selected pass@1: on MBPP+ GPT-4o-mini goes 73.5 (pass@1) to 83.8 (repeated sampling @200) to 91.0 (PlanSearch @200); DeepSeek-Coder-V2 76.3 to 81.9 to 92.6; on LiveCodeBench Claude 3.5 Sonnet 41.4 to 60.6 to 77.0. No model at or below 3B was tested [>3B]. — [Wang et al. 2024, PlanSearch](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.03733)
- Adaptive-Consistency stops sampling early when the samples drawn so far agree; over 17 reasoning and code datasets it cuts the sample budget by up to 7.9x with under 0.1% average accuracy drop. — [Aggarwal et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11860)

**Selection by execution (no repair)**
- MBR-exec: choose the program whose outputs on a few test inputs agree with the most other samples; needs inputs only. Codex on MBPP: greedy 47.3, MBR-exec with 25 samples 58.2 [>3B]. — [Shi et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.11454)
- CodeT (model writes tests, dual execution agreement, 100 code samples): HumanEval pass@1 code-davinci-002 47.0 to 65.8; InCoder-6B 16.4 to 20.6; CodeGen-Mono-16B 29.7 to 36.7. MBPP: davinci-002 58.1 to 67.7; InCoder-6B 21.3 to 34.4; CodeGen-16B 42.4 to 49.5. Tests written by the weaker models are less accurate and more often "toxic" (pass a wrong solution, fail the reference), and the gain tracks test quality; swapping in davinci-002's tests adds a further 6.2 to 15.9 points for InCoder and CodeGen [>3B]. — [Chen et al. 2022, CodeT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10397)
- DOCE, CodeLlama-7B-Instruct with 50 candidates [>3B], on HumanEval+ / MBPP-S+: greedy 39.0 / 44.8; oracle over the 50 76.2 / 72.0; filter on the prompt's trial tests only 59.7 / 60.8; MBR-exec only 49.5 / 57.8; filter plus MBR-exec 70.1 / 69.3; plus self-debugging every candidate before reranking 72.3 / 72.1. Best sampling temperature was 1.6-1.8 on HumanEval+/MBPP+ (1.2-1.4 on LiveCodeBench). MBR-exec reaches its peak with 5 test inputs on MBPP-S+ and 20 on HumanEval+. The study assumes access to the inputs of the hidden tests. — [Li et al. 2024, DOCE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.13745)
- S* (16 samples at temperature 0.7, 2 rounds of debugging on public tests, then pairwise selection on synthesized distinguishing inputs), LiveCodeBench v2, Qwen2.5-Coder-Instruct 0.5B / 1.5B / 3B / 7B: zero-shot 1.2 / 7.0 / 18.4 / 29.4; majority vote 2.5 / 11.0 / 25.2 / 40.5; self-debugging 2.4 / 9.4 / 27.8 / 39.9; S* 10.9 / 27.6 / 42.7 / 54.4 (GPT-4o-mini zero-shot 40.9). Gains saturate after 2-3 debugging rounds; adding model-generated tests to the debugging loop gave no consistent improvement; BM25-retrieved in-context examples were similar to or worse than zero-shot. Caveat: the paper uses GPT-4o-mini to generate inputs for the majority-vote clustering and for the selection ablation, so the small-model rows are not purely self-contained. — [Li et al. 2025, S*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14382)
- DiffCodeGen (May 2026): generate 18 candidates, synthesize inputs by coverage-guided fuzzing with no LLM, cluster by behaviour, return the medoid of the largest cluster; no public tests used. LiveCodeBench v2, Qwen2.5-Coder-Instruct 0.5B / 1.5B / 3B / 7B: majority vote 2.5 / 11.0 / 25.9 / 37.5; S* with public tests removed ("SkyCoder", official code) 2.9 / 12.5 / 22.6 / 35.6; DiffCodeGen 5.1 / 13.5 / 26.2 / 38.9. It uses about 4% of the input tokens of prior methods. Splitting candidates into 2 clusters was best; 293 tasks had a largest-cluster share of at least 90% and 22 below 60%. — [He et al. 2026, DiffCodeGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20473)
- Best-of-16 with 16 self-written unit tests, MBPP (unit-test accuracy / one-shot code accuracy / best-of-N accuracy): Qwen2.5-7B-Coder-Instruct 41.3 / 68.0 / 79.6; Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct 35.8 / 66.3 / 79.4; Qwen2.5-14B-Coder-Instruct 75.1 / 72.6 / 84.9; after co-training coder and tester with RL (ReasonFlux-Coder-7B) 79.4 / 70.2 / 84.6; Qwen3-4B with long reasoning 76.5 / 88.4 / 90.1 [>3B]. — [Wang et al. 2025, CURE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03136)
- B4 derives a Bayesian selection rule for "plausible solutions against plausible tests" and reports up to 12% average relative improvement over the strongest heuristic (CodeT-style), and 50% in the hardest cases with few correct solutions; tested with CodeLlama-7B-Python, DeepSeek-Coder-6.7B, StarCoder, CodeGen-16B and Codex [>3B]. — [Chen et al. 2024, B4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08692)
- Coder-Reviewer reranking (score p(program | instruction) times p(instruction | program)) gives up to 17 points absolute over coder-only likelihood reranking and, with an executability filter, can beat MBR. — [Zhang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.16490)
- LEVER trains a small verifier (T5-base for the open models) on description, program and execution result; with InCoder-6B Spider goes 24.1 (greedy) to 54.1 and GSM8K 3.1 to 11.9; with CodeGen-16B 24.6 to 51.0 and 7.1 to 22.1; with Codex MBPP goes 62.0 to 68.9 [>3B generators, 220M-class verifier]. — [Ni et al. 2023, LEVER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08468)
- A slow but simple reference program can serve as the oracle: LLM-written brute-force oracles were correct in 88% of cases and raised one-submission pass rate 8x over Codex and 2.6x over CodeT. — [Zhang et al. 2023, ALGO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14591)
- Labelling test outputs by agreement among several independent solutions ("mutual verification") reached 96.8% output-labelling accuracy. — [Liu et al. 2025, rStar-Coder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21297)
- Selecting across candidates from different systems beats the best single system (66.2% on SWE-bench Verified from an ensemble of top submissions) [>3B]. — [Ehrlich et al. 2025, CodeMonkeys](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14723)

**Do small models write useful tests?**
- Accuracy of self-written tests on HumanEval / MBPP: Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct inputs valid 97.19% / 94.35%, expected outputs correct 84.85% / 77.33%, whole suite correct 44.50% / 44.44%; GPT-4o 97.63 / 94.81, 89.77 / 85.60, 59.15 / 58.73 [>3B]. — [Chen et al., ACL 2025, Revisit Self-Debugging](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12793)
- Debugging against self-written tests hurts on basic problems: Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct HE+ 81.7 falls to 78.0 / 79.3 after one / two rounds with pass-fail feedback and to 76.2 / 75.6 with detailed feedback; MBPP+ 70.6 falls to 69.8 / 69.8 and 68.0 / 69.0. With oracle tests the same model gains: HE+ 82.9 (pass-fail feedback) or 84.1 (detailed, two rounds), MBPP+ 73.8 or 74.3 [>3B]. — [Chen et al., ACL 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12793)
- AgentCoder's separate test-designer agent reaches 89.6% / 91.4% test accuracy on HumanEval / MBPP, but with GPT-4, at 56.9K / 66.3K tokens per task [>3B]. — [Huang et al. 2023, AgentCoder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.13010)

**Repair and debugging**
- Self-Debugging on MBPP with one unit test shown in the prompt: Codex 61.4 to 70.8, GPT-3.5 67.6 to 74.2, GPT-4 72.8 to 80.6, StarCoder-15.5B 47.2 to 53.2 [>3B]. — [Chen et al. 2023, Self-Debug](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05128)
- LDB (block-level runtime states) on HumanEval / MBPP: GPT-3.5 73.8 to 82.9 / 67.6 to 76.0; CodeLlama-34B 49.4 to 55.5 / 51.2 to 57.4; StarCoder-15B 39.0 to 39.6 / 51.6 to 55.4. Cost on HumanEval: about 23K tokens and 6.2 turns per problem with GPT-3.5, 52K tokens and 7.8 turns with CodeLlama. Debugging starts only when a visible test fails [>3B]. — [Zhong et al. 2024, LDB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16906)
- Self-repair is "not a silver bullet": at equal budget, independent resampling is often as good or better, especially with small budgets; the bottleneck is the model's feedback on its own code (replacing CodeLlama-13B's feedback with GPT-3.5/GPT-4 feedback beats both baselines) [>3B]. — [Olausson et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09896)
- A 2026 study finds repair helps even at 8B: Llama-3.1-8B HumanEval 67.1 to 76.8 and MBPP-sanitized 55.6 to 71.6 over four rounds, with two rounds capturing 76-95% of the gain and about 19.9K tokens per point gained. Caveat: the feedback is the error from the benchmark's own test cases, i.e. oracle tests [>3B]. — [Arimbur 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.10508)
- At 1-1.5B, prompting for self-correction without execution feedback does not work: Qwen2.5-1.5B on MBPP 43.8 to 44.6 with a simple "revise" prompt, 43.8 to 38.2 with Self-Refine; when its second answer is wrong it is nearly identical to the first (edit distance at most 0.05) in 93% of cases (Gemini: 32%); as a verifier of its own code it is near random. RL training (CoCoS) is needed to get 45.0 to 54.2. — [Cho et al. 2025, CoCoS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23060)
- Reflexion reports 91% on HumanEval with GPT-4 (baseline 80%) [>3B]; a later analysis notes that it debugs on self-generated tests but checks the pre-repair code with hidden oracle tests. — [Shinn et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366); [Chen et al., ACL 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12793)

**Flows and agents**
- AlphaCodium: GPT-4 pass@5 on CodeContests validation 19% to 44%, using about 15-20 LLM calls per solution (about 100 for a pass@5 submission) [>3B]. — [Ridnik et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08500)
- MapCoder (retrieval, planning, coding, debugging agents): 93.9% HumanEval and 83.1% MBPP with the strongest backbone; the authors list the large token count as a limitation for resource-constrained settings; also run with Mistral-7B [>3B]. — [Islam et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11403)
- For maths (not code), search guided by a process reward model lets a 1B model exceed a 405B model on MATH-500. — [Liu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06703); [Snell et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314)

### Inferences
- The S* versus DiffCodeGen tables for the same Qwen2.5-Coder sizes isolate the value of the visible tests: with public tests 1.5B reaches 27.6 and 3B 42.7; without them 12.5-13.5 and 22.6-26.2. For small models the trusted example is worth more than any clever selector, which matches this project's result that the engine plus one example already carries much of the pipeline.
- The remaining failure mode here (passes the visible example, fails hidden tests) cannot be fixed by repair loops, because they only trigger on a visible failure, and should not be attacked with model-written expected outputs (77-85% per-test accuracy at 7B, lower at 1.5B). What the literature supports is more and better probe inputs plus behavioural agreement (MBR-exec, DOCE, DiffCodeGen), an independent oracle when one exists (ALGO; here, the rule engine), and a learned verifier over execution results (LEVER).
- DOCE's numbers suggest the ceiling: for a 7B model on MBPP-S+, filter plus MBR-exec recovers 69.3 of an oracle 72.0 at 50 candidates. The useful diagnostic for this project is therefore coverage@N of the 1.5B model on the dev split: if coverage is far above 102/151 the problem is selection; if not, it is generation and only a different generator (second model family, engine, retrieval) helps.
- Cost at an assumed 16 tok/s: a one-line answer of 40-80 tokens costs 3-5 s; 16 samples about 1 minute; LDB-style debugging (23-52K tokens per problem) or AgentCoder (57-66K) would take tens of minutes per task even if most tokens are cheaper prompt tokens. Multi-agent flows are out of budget; adaptive sampling with early stopping is the cheap lever.
- Higher temperatures than usual (DOCE: 1.6 for a 7B model on MBPP+) raise oracle and reranked accuracy when a filter exists; S* found 0.7 better on harder LiveCodeBench tasks. Temperature should be tuned on the dev split with the filter in place.

### Gaps
- No paper reports CodeT, MBR-exec, LDB, AlphaCodium, MapCoder or PlanSearch with a model at or below 3B on HumanEval+/MBPP+; the only ≤3B execution-feedback results found are S* and DiffCodeGen (LiveCodeBench) and CoCoS (MBPP, no execution feedback).
- Test-writing accuracy for 0.5B-3B models was not found; the 7B figures are an upper bound.
- No measurement of these techniques in CPU seconds was found; all cost figures are tokens or API calls.
- Whether the S* small-model rows used the small model itself or GPT-4o-mini for input synthesis is not stated in the passages read.

## 3. Cascades and routing with a free symbolic first stage

### Takeaway
Every published cascade rests on one component, a quality estimate for the cheap stage's answer, with thresholds tuned on a labelled in-distribution validation split; reported savings at equal accuracy range from 26% (code, self-testing) to 98% (FrugalGPT's best case). For code the signal that works is execution agreement, not model self-assessment (a 1.5B model is near random as a verifier of its own code), so a rule engine should enter the cascade through its conditional precision given agreement signals, not through the fact that it returned a program.

### Cited Findings
- FrugalGPT: a cascade is a scoring function g(query, answer) in [0,1], an ordered list of models and one threshold per stage; the scorer is a small regression model (DistilBERT in the case study); the model list and thresholds are learned jointly on a training split by constrained optimisation (maximise quality subject to average cost under a budget), with cascade length 3. Result: matches the best single model (GPT-4) with up to 98% lower cost, or +4% accuracy at equal cost. Stated limitation: needs labelled examples from the same distribution as the test queries. — [Chen, Zaharia, Zou 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176)
- AutoMix: the small model answers, a few-shot self-verification prompt (framed as entailment against the context) gives a noisy confidence, and either a threshold or a POMDP router decides whether to escalate; over 50% cost reduction for comparable performance on five models and five datasets. — [Aggarwal et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12963)
- RouteLLM: a router predicts the probability that the strong model's answer would be preferred; a cost threshold alpha turns that into a routing decision and is calibrated to hit a chosen share of strong-model calls; evaluated with "performance gap recovered" and "call-performance threshold" metrics; routers trained on human preference data plus augmentation cut cost by over 2x in some cases without quality loss and transfer when the model pair is changed. — [Ong et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18665)
- Hybrid LLM: a router trained to predict the quality gap between the small and large model (BART score), with a threshold that can be moved at test time; up to 40% fewer large-model calls with no quality drop, and 22% fewer calls to GPT-3.5-turbo with under 1% drop when the small model is Llama-2-13B. — [Ding et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14618)
- Token-level uncertainty: whole-sequence probability is biased by output length; learned post-hoc deferral rules over token-level uncertainty beat simple aggregation, and adding the small model's embeddings helps further (FLAN-T5 experiments). — [Gupta et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10136)
- Consistency as the deferral signal: agreement among the weak model's sampled answers, including across two representations (chain-of-thought and program-of-thought), decides escalation; comparable to GPT-4 alone at 40% of its cost on six reasoning datasets. — [Yue et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03094)
- Theory: an optimal cascading rule and a combined "cascade routing" strategy are derived; the analysis identifies the quality estimator as the critical factor for any model-selection scheme. — [Dekoninck et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10347)
- Code-specific cascade: each model writes k solutions and k tests of up to l assert lines; solutions are scored CodeT-style (number of passed test lines times how many solutions pass those lines); if the best score is below a global threshold theta times the number of valid test lines, the task goes to the next larger model. The per-model number of solutions k in {-1, 0, 1, 3, 5, 10}, the test lines l in {0, 2, 4} and the global theta in {0.0, 0.1, ..., 1.0} are chosen as Pareto-optimal points on a validation set of matching difficulty. Cost falls 26% on average and up to 70% at equal accuracy. Context from the same paper: WizardCoder-Python 7B solves 56.7% of HumanEval and 34B 72.6% at about 10x the cost; of 164 problems 75 are solved by all three sizes, 2 only by the 7B, 33 by none [>3B]. — [Chen et al. 2024, Model Cascading for Code](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15842)
- EcoAssistant: a hierarchy of cheaper-to-dearer assistants, each talking to a code executor, plus retrieval of past successful solutions as demonstrations, beats GPT-4 by 10 points of success rate at under 50% of its cost. — [Zhang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03046)
- Sample budgets can be adaptive: stopping when the samples so far agree cuts the number of samples by up to 7.9x with under 0.1% accuracy loss. — [Aggarwal et al. 2023, Adaptive-Consistency](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11860)
- Behavioural consensus as a confidence measure: with candidates clustered by behaviour, tasks whose largest cluster holds at least 90% of candidates are labelled high-confidence and under 60% low-confidence (293 and 22 tasks in the reported analysis). — [He et al. 2026, DiffCodeGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20473)
- Self-verification is not available at this size: Qwen2.5-1.5B used as verifier of its own MBPP solutions is near random, while a GPT-4.1 verifier reaches about 70% accuracy; only the external verifier produced a gain (39.3 to 49.8). — [Cho et al. 2025, CoCoS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23060)

### Inferences
- How the engine fits. The engine is a stage with near-zero marginal cost (1-2 s, no tokens), so the FrugalGPT objective says: accept its answer whenever the estimated probability that it is correct on hidden tests exceeds what the next stage would achieve net of cost. The estimate must be conditional. The project's own finding (programs that fit the single example but fail hidden tests) means "engine returned a program" has precision well below 1, while "engine program agrees with an independent model sample on probe inputs" has higher precision; that is exactly the signal that lifted the pipeline from 97-98 to 102.
- Free signals worth calibrating on the dev split before any model call: the number of behaviourally distinct engine programs that fit the example (if all fit-candidates agree on probe inputs the example was not ambiguous), program size, and whether the description's words match the operators used. Signals costing one call: agreement with one greedy 1.5B sample.
- Threshold recipe taken from the code cascade paper and FrugalGPT: sweep one or two coarse thresholds (for example largest-cluster share in {0.5, 0.7, 0.9}) on train-side tasks and keep the Pareto-optimal points. With about 150 tasks per split the standard error of an accuracy estimate is about 4 points, so fine-grained thresholds would be fitted to noise.
- Suggested order: engine; if engine candidates are unanimous on probes and one greedy sample agrees, stop; otherwise draw 3-4 samples at higher temperature, cluster engine candidates and samples together, stop if the largest cluster share passes the threshold; otherwise continue to 8-16 samples; only then, optionally, a second model family or a larger model for the low-confidence remainder.
- Not recommended at 1.5B: prompt-based self-verification (AutoMix style), and uncalibrated token-probability thresholds. If a learned scorer is wanted (the FrugalGPT DistilBERT role), the tiny from-scratch model can play it, reading description, program and probe input/output rows as in LEVER.
- A further confirmation signal to test: whether the engine can re-synthesise the model's Python answer from that answer's own input/output behaviour. A model answer that equals a short composition of known operators is less likely to be an accident of one example; this also produces training pairs for section 5.

### Gaps
- No cascade paper was found with an enumerative or rule-based first stage; the fit of the engine is an inference from the general cascade results.
- No published method for calibrating the precision of programming-by-example output from a single example was found; conformal or selective-prediction methods were not surveyed.
- The share of tasks on which the project's engine and model agree immediately (which determines the saving) is not known to me.

## 4. Neuro-symbolic hybrids and LLM tool or library making

### Takeaway
The hybrids with measured gains use the language model as a prior over an existing search space and leave correctness to the symbolic search and the examples: 58% of tasks solved against 40% for unguided search and 6% for the LLM alone (HySynth), and 80.1% against 49% LLM-only and 31% plain enumeration on SyGuS. The evidence that LLM-written libraries are reused on new tasks is weak: an audit found 3 successful reuses in 3,201 test questions for TroVE and one lemma reused once for LEGO-Prover, and ablations without reuse did as well.

### Cited Findings
- HySynth: sample 100 programs per task from GPT-4o, fit a task-specific probabilistic context-free grammar to them ("non-strict mode" also uses syntactically invalid samples), and use it to order a bottom-up enumerative search. Across ARC, tensor manipulation and string manipulation it solves 58% of tasks, unguided search 40%, direct LLM sampling 6%; it also beats the domain synthesizers Arga, TFCoder and Probe. Using 10 samples instead of 100 changes little (9 fewer string problems). Sampling 100 solutions took 4-20 s per task via API [>3B]. — [Barke et al. 2024, HySynth](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15880)
- LLM-guided enumeration on 609 SyGuS benchmarks: GPT-3.5 alone solves 49% (about 4 attempts on average for those it solves), a plain enumerator 31.0%, enumeration weighted by a grammar learned from the LLM's failed attempts 52.9% (A* variant), LLM plus that enumerator 80.1%, the state-of-the-art solver cvc5 68.1%. The stated hypothesis is that when the LLM is wrong, the correct program is usually in the neighbourhood of its wrong answers [>3B]. — [Li, Parsert, Polgreen 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03997)
- Multi-modal program inference: candidate programs from GPT-3 (given the description) supply components and a similarity guide for component-based synthesis against the examples. Regular expressions: 90% of the DeepRegex-style cases solved, brute-force search 41%, GPT-3 alone 18%; CSS selectors: 75% against 56% and 20% [>3B]. — [Rahmani et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.02445)
- Jigsaw (Pandas): program-analysis post-processing of GPT-3/Codex output (variable-name fixes, argument and semantic repair using the input/output example) improves accuracy by 15-40%; variable-name transformation alone gives 10-30% for Codex; choosing few-shot context by similarity is about 5% better than no context before post-processing and up to 15% after [>3B]. — [Jain et al. 2022, Jigsaw](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02969)
- Hypothesis search for inductive tasks: the LLM proposes natural-language hypotheses, implements them as Python, and the examples filter them; 30% on a 100-task ARC subset against 17% for direct prompting [>3B]. — [Wang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05660)
- Constrained generation into a formal language without fine-tuning: grammar-constrained decoding guarantees outputs follow a grammar and can beat task-specific fine-tuned models on structured tasks; Synchromesh adds constrained semantic decoding and example retrieval by target-program similarity; grammar prompting has the model first predict a minimal grammar for the task. — [Geng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13971); [Poesia et al. 2022, Synchromesh](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11227); [Wang et al. 2023, Grammar Prompting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19234)
- llama.cpp supports grammar-constrained sampling: GBNF grammars constrain model output in its command-line, completion and server tools, so a quantized small model can be forced to emit only strings of a given formal language. — [llama.cpp grammars README](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/grammars/README.md)
- Tool making. LATM: GPT-4 writes reusable tools once, GPT-3.5 uses them, matching GPT-4 in both roles at lower cost on Big-Bench-style task families. CREATOR: separating abstract tool creation from concrete use improves MATH and TabMWP. CRAFT: tools abstracted from GPT-4 solutions of training examples, retrieved at inference. TroVE: a toolbox grown and trimmed online, with CodeLlama-7B, gives simpler and more accurate solutions on 11 datasets with 79-98% smaller toolboxes. ReGAL: refactoring existing programs into shared functions raises CodeLlama-13B accuracy by 11.5 points on LOGO, 26.1 on date understanding and 8.1 on TextCraft [>3B]. — [LATM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17126); [CREATOR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14318); [CRAFT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17428); [TroVE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12869); [ReGAL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16467)
- LILO combines LLM-guided synthesis with symbolic compression (Stitch) and auto-documentation of the learned abstractions; the documentation step itself improves the synthesizer's use of the abstractions; compared against DreamCoder on string editing, scene reasoning and graphics. — [Grand et al. 2023, LILO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19791); [Ellis et al. 2020, DreamCoder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08381)
- "Library learning doesn't": TroVE's final libraries on MATH hold 15 functions, of which 2 are reused in a correct solution (3 successful reuses in 3,201 test questions); disabling library import while keeping the 15 sampled solutions matches or beats TroVE on three of four splits tested. In LEGO-Prover's logs about 6% of learned lemmas reach the prover, exactly one is reused, once; an ablation without cross-problem sharing performs similarly. The authors attribute the reported gains to self-correction and self-consistency. — [Berlot-Attwell, Rudzicz, Si 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20274)

### Inferences
- The audit matches this project's observation that operators written by a strong teacher do not transfer to unseen tasks. The tool-making papers that report gains (LATM, CRAFT, ReGAL, TroVE) evaluate on tasks from the same family as the ones the tools were made from, and where reuse was actually counted it was nearly absent.
- What transfers is the direction of HySynth, Rahmani et al. and Li et al.: per-task guidance over a fixed operator set. For this system: take the Python samples the pipeline already draws (HySynth needed only about 10), map their calls, operators and constants to engine operators, and reweight or restrict the enumeration for that task. Samples that fail the example are still usable (HySynth's non-strict mode; Li et al.'s neighbourhood hypothesis). This could make depth-3 search affordable inside the 1-2 s budget on tasks where the model's samples are near misses.
- The reverse direction is Jigsaw: treat the model's best program as a sketch and let a symbolic search repair it locally (constants, argument order, off-by-one, operator swaps) against the example and probe agreement.
- Having the small model write the engine's language directly under a grammar constraint is possible but unpromising as a first experiment: HySynth's motivating observation is that even GPT-4o rarely writes a fully correct program in an unfamiliar DSL (6% without search). Keeping the model in Python and crossing languages through behaviour (section 5) uses the model where it is strong.

### Gaps
- None of the LLM-guided synthesis papers used a model at or below 3B, and none targets Python-level MBPP tasks; expected gains for this system are extrapolations.
- Reuse audits exist only for mathematics (MATH, miniF2F); no audit of tool reuse for programming tasks was found.
- No measurement was found of how good a 1.5B model's samples are as a prior over a foreign operator set.

## 5. Distilling a small pretrained code model into a tiny student

### Takeaway
Published students that keep most of a teacher's accuracy on open-ended Python generation have 350M parameters or more (phi-1-small reaches 45% HumanEval at 350M against 50.6% at 1.3B); the results that keep 96-99% at 3 MB or roughly 15M parameters are for classification. Below 100M parameters the successes are on narrow closed distributions (a 60M model for Excel formulas, sub-10M models for a synthetic story world) and come from large volumes of targeted synthetic data, which is the form of distillation that fits a 12M model writing one-line DSL programs.

### Cited Findings
- Logit distillation: a small model is trained on the teacher's softened output distribution; introduced for compressing ensembles and large acoustic models. — [Hinton, Vinyals, Dean 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531)
- DistilBERT: 40% smaller, 60% faster, keeps 97% of BERT's language-understanding score. TinyBERT (4 layers): 7.5x smaller and 9.4x faster than BERT-base (about 14.5M parameters by that ratio), keeps 96.8% on GLUE, using layer-wise distillation at both pre-training and task stages. MiniLM: distils the last layer's self-attention; keeps over 99% on SQuAD 2.0 and several GLUE tasks with half the parameters. All classification or span-extraction tasks. — [DistilBERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01108); [TinyBERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10351); [MiniLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10957)
- Sequence-level distillation for generation: training the student on the teacher's decoded outputs gives a student 10x faster with little loss, +4.2 / +1.7 BLEU over the same student without distillation (greedy / beam), and with pruning 13x fewer parameters for -0.4 BLEU; it also removes most of the need for beam search. — [Kim and Rush 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07947)
- Distilling step-by-step: teacher rationales used as an extra training target; a 770M T5 beats few-shot 540B PaLM using 80% of the data on one benchmark, students up to 2000x smaller than the teacher, and 220M students beat standard fine-tuning with as little as 12.5% of the examples. — [Hsieh et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02301)
- Symbolic knowledge distillation: the teacher's knowledge is written out as text, filtered by a trained critic, and used to train a student of a different type; the student is 100x smaller and surpasses the teacher on the distilled skill. — [West et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.07178)
- Code models, classification: Compressor shrinks CodeBERT and GraphCodeBERT to 3 MB (160x smaller, 4.31x and 4.15x faster) keeping 96.15% and 97.74% on vulnerability prediction and 99.20% and 97.52% on clone detection, by architecture search plus distillation on unlabelled data. Avatar takes the same models from 481 MB to 3 MB with up to 76x lower latency, up to 184x less energy and 1.67% average loss. Neither evaluates code generation. — [Shi et al. 2022, Compressor](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07120); [Shi et al. 2023, Avatar](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.04076)
- Code models, generation by data distillation: a 350M model trained on GPT-3.5 outputs mimics it on code summarisation; personalised distillation (student tries first, teacher corrects) with 2.5-3K examples costing 4-6 USD lifts CodeGen-mono-16B by 7 points to 36.4% HumanEval and StarCoder by 12.2 to 45.8%, beating standard distillation with one third of the data; SODA reports a 65.96% relative gain in average pass@1 for about 7B students [>3B]. — [Su and McMillan 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14731); [Chen et al. 2023, Personalised Distillation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18628); [Chen et al. 2024, SODA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03680)
- From-scratch small code models: phi-1 (1.3B) trained on about 6B tokens of filtered code plus under 1B tokens of GPT-3.5-written textbooks, then fine-tuned on about 180M tokens of GPT-3.5-written exercises, reaches 50.6% HumanEval and 55.5% MBPP; before the exercise fine-tuning it scores 29%; phi-1-small (350M, same pipeline) 45%; a 350M model on unfiltered code saturates at 12.19%. — [Gunasekar et al. 2023, phi-1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11644)
- Narrow domains at tiny scale: FLAME, 60M parameters trained only on Excel formulas with a formula-specific tokenizer, beats Codex-Davinci (175B), Codex-Cushman (12B) and CodeT5 (220M) in 10 of 14 repair and completion settings. TinyStories shows models under 10M parameters (released sizes 1M-33M) writing fluent, consistent stories when trained on a GPT-generated corpus restricted to a small vocabulary. — [Joshi et al. 2023, FLAME](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13779); [Eldan and Li 2023, TinyStories](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759)
- Small models made to do one thing by training rather than prompting: after RL fine-tuning, Qwen2.5-1.5B's MBPP accuracy goes from 45.0 on the first attempt to 54.2 on the second, where prompting the untrained model gave 43.8 to 44.6; 580K verified reasoning examples take Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B from 6.5 to 40.1 on LCB (inferred base from the reported +33.6). — [CoCoS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23060); [rStar-Coder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21297)
- Qwen3's small models are produced by "strong-to-weak" distillation; in the report's comparison on an 8B student, on-policy distillation from teacher logits beats reinforcement learning at about one tenth of the GPU hours [>3B]. — [Qwen3 report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09388)
- Direction of generation matters for synthetic data: instruction-tuned code models are better at turning code into an instruction than the reverse, and generating instructions for existing code then retraining improves the models (InverseCoder). Program-first synthesis scales: SynSQL-2.5M has 2.5M samples over 16,000 synthetic databases, each with SQL, question and reasoning; ARC models trained on synthetic variations of programs that solve the training tasks approach human-level when induction and transduction are ensembled. — [Wu et al. 2024, InverseCoder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05700); [Li et al. 2025, OmniSQL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.02240); [Li et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02272)
- Verification of synthetic labels by agreement: outputs labelled by agreement among independent solutions were 96.8% accurate; LLM-written brute-force oracles were correct 88% of the time. — [rStar-Coder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21297); [ALGO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14591)
- A learned verifier can be far smaller than the generator: LEVER's verifier is T5-base-sized and lifts 6B-16B generators by 20-30 points on Spider. — [Ni et al. 2023, LEVER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08468)

### Inferences
- Size versus retention, as far as the literature goes: classification, 3 MB to 15M parameters keep 96-99%; translation-like generation, 10-13x compression costs under 1 BLEU; open-ended Python from descriptions, 350M keeps about 89% of a 1.3B sibling's HumanEval (45 against 50.6) and nothing smaller is reported; closed DSL or restricted language, 10-60M is enough. A 12M student should therefore be asked to cover a closed operator vocabulary, not general Python.
- Logit and hidden-state distillation do not apply across this teacher-student pair: the teacher's distribution is over Python subword tokens, the student's over DSL tokens, with different tokenizers and widths. What can cross is (a) the teacher's behaviour, turned into programs in the student's language, (b) ranking or verification labels, and (c) descriptions.
- Three ways to cross the language gap, most credible first. (1) Teacher as oracle, engine as translator: for an unlabeled description, take the pipeline's consensus Python solution, run it on many probe inputs, and give that many-example specification to the engine; the engine's programming-by-example is far less ambiguous with 20 examples than with 1, and any DSL program it finds is verified against the teacher's behaviour. Output: (description, DSL program) pairs plus operator statistics. This is Kim-and-Rush sequence-level distillation with a symbolic translation step, and ALGO/rStar-style agreement as the filter. (2) Program first: sample engine compositions, execute them, have the small model write the one-sentence description (the easier direction per InverseCoder), then keep a pair only if the model, given its own description, writes Python that agrees with the DSL program on probes. This scales to 10^5-10^6 pairs, the order of magnitude that phi-1's exercises, TinyStories and SynSQL used, and directly targets the "a few thousand examples is not enough" limit. (3) Constrained decoding so the pretrained model emits DSL itself; weakest, see section 4.
- Personalised distillation suggests spending teacher calls only on tasks the engine and tiny coder currently fail, and distilling step-by-step suggests giving the tiny coder an auxiliary target (for example the set of operators, or a sketch) before the program.
- The cheapest thing to distil may be the selector, not the generator: label the 1.5B model's candidates on train-side tasks with hidden tests and train the tiny model as a LEVER-style verifier over description, program and probe rows.

### Gaps
- No paper was found on distilling a description-to-Python teacher into a description-to-DSL student; recipes (1) and (2) are assembled from adjacent work.
- No HumanEval/MBPP result was found for any model under 100M parameters, so there is no published retention figure at the 12M scale.
- Whether a 1.5B model's one-sentence descriptions of sampled DSL programs match the style of MBPP descriptions closely enough to transfer is untested; phi-1 and TinyStories used GPT-3.5/GPT-4 as the writer.

## 6. Retrieval augmentation as a substitute for parameters

### Takeaway
Retrieval replaces parameters when the datastore contains close neighbours of what must be produced: RETRO matches models with 25x more parameters using a 2-trillion-token store, and a weak code model gains 12 points on HumanEval from the top-5 BM25-retrieved solved problems. For basic programming tasks BM25 is as good as dense retrievers; general sources (tutorials, documentation, StackOverflow, GitHub) gave no gain, and a stronger instruct model gained nothing. Static embeddings reach about 75-82% of MiniLM-L6's retrieval score at 80-300x the speed, but at this datastore size speed is not the constraint.

### Cited Findings
- kNN-LM: interpolating a trained LM with nearest neighbours in its own embedding space cuts WikiText-103 perplexity by 2.9 to 15.79 with no further training, and the datastore can be swapped for domain adaptation; the authors conclude that learning similarity between contexts is easier than predicting the next word. — [Khandelwal et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00172)
- RETRO: conditioning on chunks retrieved with a frozen BERT retriever from a 2-trillion-token database gives performance comparable to GPT-3 and Jurassic-1 on the Pile with 25x fewer parameters; existing models can be retrofitted. — [Borgeaud et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04426)
- Retrieve-and-edit: retrieve a training example with a task-specific learned embedding, then edit it; improves a sequence-to-sequence baseline on Python autocomplete and Hearthstone card code. RECODE: retrieve similar descriptions and boost the decoder's probability of syntax-tree fragments from their code, up to +2.6 BLEU. — [Hashimoto et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01194); [Hayati et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10025)
- ReACC (code completion): a hybrid of BM25 and a dense retriever built on GraphCodeBERT feeds similar code to the generator; state of the art on CodeXGLUE completion at the time. REDCODER: dense retrieval of code or summaries as extra input for generation and summarisation. RepoCoder: iterative retrieval and generation improves in-file completion by over 10%. — [Lu et al. 2022, ReACC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07722); [Parvez et al. 2021, REDCODER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.11601); [Zhang et al. 2023, RepoCoder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12570)
- DocPrompting: retrieve documentation, then generate. CodeT5 on CoNaLa gains 2.85 points pass@1 (52% relative) and 4.39 pass@10; on the Bash dataset tldr, with a BM25 retriever and top-10 documents, CodeT5 and GPT-Neo-1.3B gain up to 6.9 points exact match. — [Zhou et al. 2023, DocPrompting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05987)
- CodeRAG-Bench, retrieval quality (NDCG@10 averaged over code-generation datasets): BM25 57.7, GIST-base 58.0, BGE-base 58.8, BGE-large 56.3, Codesage-small 60.1, GIST-large 61.7, OpenAI-03 61.7, Voyage-code 63.7, Jina-v2-code 65.4. On the HumanEval and MBPP "problem solution" store every retriever scores 96-100 (BM25 100.0 and 98.6). Cost: BM25 0.15 ms encoding and 0.02 ms search with a 141 MB index; GIST-base 3.7 ms and 9.7 ms with a 440 MB model. — [Wang et al. 2024, CodeRAG-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14497)
- CodeRAG-Bench, end-to-end, top-5 documents prepended, the task's own answer removed from the store: StarCoder2-7B (a base completion model) HumanEval 31.7 without retrieval, 43.9 with BM25, 38.7-39.0 with GIST-large, Voyage or OpenAI embeddings; MBPP rises to 51.8 with BM25 and 50.4-52.6 with dense retrievers from a very low no-context score (the paper lists 2.4 in one table and 10.4 in another). DeepSeekCoder-7B-instruct gains nothing: HumanEval 70.1 to 68.9, MBPP 60.8 to 60.0 with BM25 [>3B]. — [Wang et al. 2024, CodeRAG-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14497)
- CodeRAG-Bench, open sources with BM25 for StarCoder2 on HumanEval: tutorials 27.4, library documentation 29.3, StackOverflow 32.9, GitHub 30.5, against 31.7 with no retrieval [>3B]. — [Wang et al. 2024, CodeRAG-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14497)
- Example retrieval inside prompts: AceCoder (retrieved similar programs plus a test-case "preliminary") improves pass@1 by up to 56.4% relative on MBPP for 6B-13B models [>3B]; Jigsaw's similarity-selected context is about 5% better than none; in S*, BM25-retrieved worked examples were similar to or worse than zero-shot while examples grouped by solution technique helped at 8 or more samples; Synchromesh trains its retriever to match descriptions whose target programs are similar, not whose wording is similar. — [Li et al. 2023, AceCoder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17780); [Jigsaw](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02969); [S*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14382); [Synchromesh](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11227)
- Static embeddings (Model2Vec): average MTEB score all-MiniLM-L6-v2 55.93, potion-base-32M 52.13, potion-base-8M 51.08, GloVe-300d 45.82, BPEmb 41.74. Retrieval sub-score: MiniLM 42.92, potion-retrieval-32M 35.06 (81.69% of MiniLM), static-retrieval-mrl-en-v1 34.95, potion-base-32M 32.67, potion-base-8M 31.11, GloVe 21.80, BPEmb 15.36. — [Model2Vec results](https://github.com/MinishLab/model2vec/blob/main/results/README.md)
- Speed in the same project's classification benchmark (samples per second): TF-IDF 93,718, fastText 96,288, Model2Vec fine-tuned 81,018, Model2Vec plus logistic regression 32,869, SetFit on MiniLM-L6 1,010, fully fine-tuned MiniLM-L6 274; that is 80x and 295x in Model2Vec's favour. Hardware is not stated on the page. — [Model2Vec results](https://github.com/MinishLab/model2vec/blob/main/results/README.md)

### Inferences
- Is BM25 enough? For "find a similar solved task" over short descriptions, yes as the first system: it matched or beat dense retrievers on HumanEval and MBPP in CodeRAG-Bench and is free to run. A neural retriever is justified only if a dev-split comparison shows it retrieves tasks with the same solution pattern more often; the S* and Synchromesh findings say the similarity that matters is of the target program, not the wording, which a general sentence encoder does not directly capture.
- When retrieval helps: when the store holds near-duplicates in the right output format and the generator is weak (StarCoder2 base: +12 on HumanEval; the engine and the 12M coder are much weaker still). When it does not: strong instruct models on basic tasks, and generic corpora. The 1.5B instruct model is between the two cases, so the expected gain for it is small; the expected gain for the tiny coder and the engine is larger.
- Uses that fit this system: (a) for the engine, retrieve the k nearest solved train-side tasks and boost their operators in the enumeration, a retrieval version of the HySynth prior that costs no model call; (b) for the tiny coder, retrieve-and-edit or a kNN-LM-style interpolation over a store of verified (description, DSL program) pairs, which lets memory stand in for parameters exactly as in RETRO; (c) for the 1.5B model, retrieved (description, example, solution) triples as few-shot demonstrations, to be kept only if the dev split shows a gain.
- Encoder choice on CPU: with 10^3-10^5 stored descriptions and one query per task, even a MiniLM-L6-class encoder costs milliseconds per query, so the static-versus-transformer decision should be made on retrieval quality (static models lose roughly 20-25% of MiniLM's retrieval score; GloVe loses half). Static embeddings or TF-IDF become interesting only for embedding millions of synthetic programs or for use inside the search loop.
- The retriever can be trained in-house on target similarity: label two train-side tasks as similar when their verified DSL programs share operators, and train the tiny model's encoder on that, following Synchromesh's target-similarity tuning.

### Gaps
- No retrieval-augmented result for a model at or below 3B on HumanEval+/MBPP+ was found; the CodeRAG-Bench generators are 7B and larger.
- The rate of near-duplicate tasks between MBPP train-side splits and the project's test split is unknown, and it bounds what retrieval can add without leakage.
- kNN-LM or RETRO-style retrieval has not been reported for sub-100M code models; the proposal for the tiny coder is an extrapolation.
- No CPU-only throughput figure for MiniLM-L6 with stated hardware was found.

## 7. Concrete recommendations: best accuracy per CPU-second, and the most credible knowledge transfer

### Takeaway
The best-supported next steps keep the 1.5B Apache-2.0 model and spend effort on selection and diversity rather than on a bigger model or repair loops: adaptive sampling at tuned temperature, behavioural clustering on better probe inputs with the engine and tiny coders as extra voters, and a coverage@N diagnostic to see whether selection or generation is the limit. The most credible knowledge transfer is behavioural: use the small model's consensus programs as oracles and its descriptions as labels, let the engine translate behaviour into DSL programs, and train the tiny coder and the engine's priors on the resulting verified pairs at 10^5 scale.

### Cited Findings
- Model choice: Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct MBPP+ 59.4 (1,117 MB at Q4_K_M, Apache-2.0); 3B 62.4 (2,105 MB, research licence); 7B 71.7; 1.5B pass@2 about equals 3B pass@1 in an independent study (0.60 against 0.59). — [Qwen2.5-Coder report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12186); [GGUF sizes](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct-GGUF); [Hasan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03160)
- Headroom from sampling: mean pass@1 to pass@10 for Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B is 0.54 to 0.68. Execution-based selection recovers most of such headroom when the filter is trustworthy: 69.3 of an oracle 72.0 on MBPP-S+ for a 7B model with 50 candidates [>3B]. — [Hasan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03160); [DOCE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.13745)
- The visible test is the main source of gain for small models: Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B on LCB v2 reaches 27.6 with S* using public tests and 12.5-13.5 with the same family of methods without them. — [S*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14382); [DiffCodeGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20473)
- Inputs can come from fuzzing with no model call and about 4% of the input tokens; model-written inputs are 94-97% valid but model-written expected outputs are 77-85% accurate per test and under 45% per suite at 7B. — [DiffCodeGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20473); [Chen et al., ACL 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12793)
- Early stopping on agreement saves up to 7.9x samples at under 0.1% accuracy loss. — [Adaptive-Consistency](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11860)
- Candidates from different systems selected together beat the best single system. — [CodeMonkeys](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14723)
- Repair at this size: prompting a 1.5B model to revise changes almost nothing (93% near-identical second answers), repair needs a failing test to start, and debugging on self-written tests lowers HE+ and MBPP+ for a 7B model. — [CoCoS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23060); [LDB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16906); [Chen et al., ACL 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12793)
- Agent flows cost 15-20 calls per solution (AlphaCodium) or 23-66K tokens per task (LDB, AgentCoder). — [AlphaCodium](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08500); [LDB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16906); [AgentCoder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.13010)
- Model as prior over a symbolic search: 58% against 40% unguided and 6% LLM-only, with about 10 samples nearly as good as 100. — [HySynth](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15880)
- LLM-made libraries are almost never reused on new problems (3 reuses in 3,201 questions). — [Berlot-Attwell et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20274)
- Tiny models succeed on closed distributions with large synthetic corpora (60M for Excel formulas; under 10M for TinyStories; 350M reaches 45% HumanEval only with about 7B training tokens). — [FLAME](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13779); [TinyStories](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759); [phi-1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11644)
- Code-to-description is the easier direction for small code models, and agreement among independent solutions labels outputs with 96.8% accuracy. — [InverseCoder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05700); [rStar-Coder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21297)
- A weak generator gains 12 HumanEval points from the top-5 BM25-retrieved solved problems; a strong instruct model gains none [>3B]. — [CodeRAG-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14497)

### Inferences
Configurations to try, ordered by expected accuracy per CPU-second (all are proposals, none is a measured result for this system):

1. Diagnostic first (no new method): on the dev split measure coverage@N of the 1.5B model for N = 1, 4, 8, 16 under the visible-example filter. If coverage@16 is well above the current selected accuracy, work on selection (items 2-4); if it is close, work on generation diversity (items 5-6).
2. Adaptive sampling with a consensus stop: engine first; one greedy sample; stop if engine candidates and the sample agree on probes; otherwise sample in small batches at a temperature tuned on dev (the literature's optimum ranges from 0.7 to 1.6 depending on task difficulty), stopping when the largest behavioural cluster passes a share threshold. Expected effect: same or better accuracy at fewer than the current 5 calls on easy tasks, more calls only where they can change the answer.
3. Better probes, no oracle outputs: type-aware random and boundary inputs generated symbolically (empty, single element, duplicates, negatives, zero, large values), optionally a few model-proposed inputs; cluster by behaviour; never trust model-written expected outputs. This is aimed directly at the "passes the example, fails hidden tests" failures, which are mostly edge-case disagreements between candidates.
4. Heterogeneous voting: put engine candidates, tiny-coder candidates and model samples into one behavioural clustering, weighting sources by their dev-split precision. Add a second small model family (OpenCoder-1.5B, Qwen3-1.7B without thinking, or Qwen2.5-Coder-0.5B as a cheap extra voter at about 2x the token rate) only if the diagnostic shows errors of the 1.5B samples are correlated.
5. Model samples as an engine prior: map the operators and constants in the model's Python samples (including failed ones) to engine operators and reweight enumeration for that task; in the other direction, let the engine repair the model's best candidate locally. Both reuse samples already paid for.
6. Retrieval for the weak components: BM25 over train-side solved tasks to boost operators in the engine and to give the tiny coder a neighbour to edit; test few-shot retrieval for the 1.5B model but expect little.
7. A LEVER-style verifier trained on train-side candidates of the 1.5B model, using the tiny architecture, as an additional score in selection.
8. Lower priority: swapping to the 3B model (about +3 MBPP+ single-shot for 2x time and a research-only licence), thinking-mode models (minutes per call), self-debug loops and multi-agent flows (no trigger for the dominant failure mode, unreliable self-written tests, 10-100x the token cost).

Knowledge transfer into the tiny coder and the engine, most credible first:

1. Behavioural distillation through the engine. Collect many unlabeled one-sentence tasks (train-side descriptions, paraphrases, and descriptions the model writes for sampled programs). For each, run the pipeline to a high-consensus Python answer, execute it on 20-50 probe inputs, and ask the engine for DSL programs matching that behaviour. Keep (description, DSL program) pairs where the engine succeeds; use them to train the 12M coder and to estimate description-word-to-operator priors for the engine. Tasks where the engine fails are a ranked list of missing operators, grounded in real behaviours instead of teacher-invented ones.
2. Program-first back-description. Sample engine compositions up to the depth the engine can search, discard degenerate ones by execution, have the 1.5B model write the description, and keep pairs that survive a round trip (model re-solves from its own description and agrees on probes). Target 10^5 or more pairs, with train-side MBPP descriptions as style examples.
3. Per-task prior at inference (item 5 above), which transfers knowledge without any training.
4. Distil the selector (item 7 above).
5. Not credible on current evidence: logit or hidden-state distillation into the 12M model (different tokenizer and output language; no published generation student below 350M), and asking any LLM to grow the operator library for unseen tasks.

### Gaps
- None of the configurations has been measured for a ≤3B model on MBPP+ in the literature; the ordering is a judgment from adjacent results and from the project's own baseline numbers.
- The gain from heterogeneous voting depends on error correlation between the engine, the tiny coders and the 1.5B model, which only the project's own logs can show.
- The yield of the engine as a translator (share of the model's consensus programs that it can re-synthesise from 20-50 examples) is unknown and decides whether transfer path 1 produces enough data.
- CPU-second costs are estimated from token counts and an assumed 16 tok/s for the 1.5B model; prompt-processing time and llama.cpp prompt caching across samples were not measured or sourced.
