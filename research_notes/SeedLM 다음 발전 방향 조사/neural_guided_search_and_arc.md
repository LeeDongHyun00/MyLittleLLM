# Neural-guided program search, execution-guided synthesis, and ARC-AGI lessons for a small from-scratch program synthesizer

Notes compiled 2026-11-23. Scope: search and test-time techniques that could extend the reach of a small (about 12M-parameter, CPU-only, a few seconds per task) program synthesizer that gets one sentence plus ONE input/output example, without flooding it with programs that fit the example by coincidence.

Reading conventions used below:
- Numbers are quoted from the paper text or tables that were actually read (arXiv abstract page, arXiv/ar5iv HTML full text, or PDF text). Where a number exists only in a figure, or a page could not be read, this is said explicitly.
- "(snippet-level)" marks a fact seen only in a search-result summary, not in the full text. Treat those as unverified.
- "Inferences" sections are this researcher's reasoning, not claims from the sources. Expected gains given there are estimates, not measurements.

## 1. Neural-guided search for programming by example, trained from scratch on synthetic data (2017-2025)

### Takeaway
The methods that reach long or novel compositions with small models are the ones that (a) guide search on executed intermediate values rather than on program text, and (b) train the guide on the states its own search visits; with these, models of at most about 13M parameters find programs of 10 to 20 nodes or statements. Plain sequence decoding with a wide beam does not generalize in length or composition (for example 90.9% in distribution versus 42.4% under compositional shift for a from-scratch Transformer), and operator-level "premise selection" gives the smallest gains of all guidance types.

#### DeepCoder (Balog, Gaunt, Brockschmidt, Nowozin, Tarlow; ICLR 2017)
- DSL of 34 functions (10 first-order, 5 higher-order, 19 lambdas), integers in -256..255; a feed-forward encoder with 3 hidden layers of 256 sigmoid units over 5 I/O examples predicts which functions occur; neither a parameter count nor a dataset size is stated — [DeepCoder, arXiv:1611.01989](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.01989)
- Speedups at program length T=3 (search space about 2x10^6) at 20% / 40% / 60% of problems solved: DFS 15.2x / 3.9x / 2.9x; sort-and-add enumeration 62.2x / 54.6x / 31.5x; lambda^2 80.4x / 94.6x / 6.2x; Sketch more than 467x at 20%. At T=5 (space about 10^10, network trained on T=4): DFS 6.8x / 5.6x / 2.6x; enumeration 907x at 20% — [DeepCoder, arXiv:1611.01989](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.01989)
- Networks trained on lengths 1..4 were tested on lengths 1..5 and "are able to generalize beyond programs of the same length that they were trained on" (values only in a figure). An RNN decoder with beam search matched the other methods only for T <= 2. The authors note that their five examples with integers up to 256 in magnitude are "probably more informative than typical (smaller) examples" — [DeepCoder, arXiv:1611.01989](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.01989)

#### RobustFill (Devlin, Uesato, Bhupatiraju, Singh, Mohamed, Kohli; ICML 2017)
- Attentional LSTM encoder-decoder (recurrent and fully connected layers of size 512, embeddings 128, 430 program tokens), trained on 2 million minibatches of 128 (256 million random programs) for about 24 hours on 2 Titan X GPUs; programs are concatenations of up to 10 expressions; 4 observed examples, 6 held-out assessment examples; parameter count not stated — [RobustFill, arXiv:1703.07469](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07469)
- Accuracy on the 205-instance FlashFill test: basic seq2seq 51% (beam 100) / 56% (beam 1000); Attention-C 83% / 86%; Attention-C-DP 89% / 92%; the previous neural approach 23% / 34% — [RobustFill, arXiv:1703.07469](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07469)
- With beam 1 the model "only generates consistent output roughly 50% of the time"; consistency with the observed examples stays "relatively constant" as examples are added while generalization rises; consistency is "a necessary, but not sufficient, condition". Program synthesis reaches 81% versus 53% for direct output induction (transduction) — [RobustFill, arXiv:1703.07469](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07469)

#### PCCoder (Zohar and Wolf; NeurIPS 2018)
- A dense network over the current program state (each variable embedded by a 56-unit SELU layer, then a 10-layer dense block) predicts the next statement, the operator, and which variable to drop (the learned garbage collector); trained on 143,000 programs of length up to 12; search is CAB with beam 100 that doubles after each pass; no parameter count — [PCCoder, arXiv:1809.04682](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04682)
- Solved within 5,000 s (two memory sizes) versus a DeepCoder reimplementation: length 5: 99.2% / 99.6% vs 63.0%; length 8: 90.0% / 91.2% vs 11.2%; length 10: 73.0% / 74.0% vs 7.0%; length 12: 65.4% / 67.2% vs 4.0%; length 14 (longer than any training program): 52.0% / 60.4% vs 2.0% — [PCCoder, arXiv:1809.04682](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04682)
- Ablation on 1,000 length-8 programs: full model 83%; random variable dropping 77%; without the auxiliary function-prediction task 70% — [PCCoder, arXiv:1809.04682](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04682)

#### Execution-guided neural program synthesis (Chen, Liu, Song; ICLR 2019)
- Karel, 1,116,854 training programs, 5 I/O pairs shown plus 1 held out; decoder is a 2-layer LSTM of size 256 fed with a CNN encoding of the state reached by executing the partial program; beam 64 — [Chen, Liu, Song, ICLR 2019 (OpenReview)](https://openreview.net/forum?id=H1gfOiAqYm)
- Generalization accuracy: baseline MLE 71.91%, baseline MLE+RL 77.12%; execution-guided 85.08% (supervised) and 86.04% (RL); with an ensemble of 15 models 91.60% to 92.00%. Exact match stays at 39-47%, i.e. the found programs are mostly not the reference program text — [Chen, Liu, Song, ICLR 2019 (OpenReview)](https://openreview.net/forum?id=H1gfOiAqYm)
- The ensemble keeps only programs consistent with all examples and breaks ties by majority vote or by the shortest program; "shortest" is better with few models, majority vote with more — [Chen, Liu, Song, ICLR 2019 (OpenReview)](https://openreview.net/forum?id=H1gfOiAqYm)

#### Write, Execute, Assess: synthesis with a REPL (Ellis, Nye, Pu, Sosa, Tenenbaum, Solar-Lezama; NeurIPS 2019)
- A policy proposes the next line and a value network scores the executed state of the partial program; search is sequential Monte Carlo that reweights and resamples particles by value; policy is a 1-layer GRU pointer network with 512 units; pretraining on random programs (about 1.7M examples in 3D, 5M in 2D) followed by REINFORCE fine-tuning — [REPL, arXiv:1906.04604](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04604)
- Largest successful programs: 19 lines / 102 tokens (3D) and 22 lines / 107 tokens (2D); the best ablations reach only 3 lines / 19 tokens (3D) and 19 lines / 87 tokens (2D). The branching factor is about 1.3 million per line in 3D — [REPL, arXiv:1906.04604](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04604)
- String editing: trained on programs of at most 30 actions (average about 8), the model regularly produced programs of 40 actions or more; SMC needs "10x fewer node expansions"; timeout 2 minutes. Exact solve rates are only in figures — [REPL, arXiv:1906.04604](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04604)

#### Property signatures (Odena and Sutton; ICLR 2020)
- A property is a boolean function of (input, output); a signature records AllTrue / AllFalse / Mixed over the examples. On 185 human-written test programs the baseline synthesizer solved 28 on average and 73 with property signatures (three runs of 100,000 s); against a DeepCoder-style predictor on 32 functions: 16.33 vs 3.33 solved — [Property signatures, arXiv:2002.09030](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09030)
- A small network predicts the signature of g from the signatures of f and f composed with g with 87.5% test accuracy — [Property signatures, arXiv:2002.09030](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09030)

#### TF-Coder (Shi, Bieber, Singh; 2020)
- 134 TensorFlow operations; weighted bottom-up enumeration with hand-set integer weights and two-stage argument filtering (filtering eliminated 98.6% of candidates for one operation in a hard example) — [TF-Coder, arXiv:2003.09040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.09040)
- 63 of 70 real tasks solved within 5 minutes with both models (17 s average), 61 with no model. Removing the weights loses 10 tasks; removing filtering loses 19. The tensor-features model alone: 63 tasks, 1.46x total speedup. The natural-language model alone: 61 tasks (no extra task), 1.08x to 1.19x total speedup. Best combination 1.59x total / 1.78x aggregate — [TF-Coder, arXiv:2003.09040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.09040)
- "Most tasks only require a single input/output example"; the largest solution has 5 operations; in the user study 5 of 18 user-written examples led to false positives, all on problems whose output had very few elements that all appear in the inputs — [TF-Coder, arXiv:2003.09040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.09040)

#### Probe: just-in-time learning (Barke, Peleg, Polikarpova; OOPSLA 2020)
- Starts from a uniform PCFG, enumerates bottom-up in order of rounded cost (negative log-probability), and updates rule probabilities from partial solutions (programs that satisfy a subset of the examples), then restarts — [Probe, arXiv:2010.08663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08663)
- Within 10 minutes: String 48 of 82 (size-based enumeration 42, EuPhony 23, CVC4 75); BitVec 21 of 27 (20, 14, 13); Circuit 22 of 31 (22, 13, 19). On an extended string grammar with 70 benchmarks: Probe 25, size-based 9, height-based 1 — [Probe, arXiv:2010.08663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08663)
- CVC4 solves more string tasks but with solutions 9 times larger on average and heavy case splitting (0.42 if-then-else per example vs 0.01 for Probe); on nine short/long string pairs Probe's programs are 100% accurate on unseen inputs versus 68.1% for CVC4 — [Probe, arXiv:2010.08663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08663)

#### BUSTLE (Odena, Shi, Bieber, Singh, Sutton, Dai; ICLR 2021)
- A feed-forward binary classifier takes the property signatures of (inputs, output) and (intermediate value, output) and predicts whether the value is a sub-expression of a solution; its output is binned into 6 levels that add 0 to 5 to the value's cost. Trained on 200,000 examples from 1,000 searches, "a few hours on CPU"; inference plus signatures cost about 20% of run time and are batched over hundreds of values; implemented in Java on one CPU — [BUSTLE, arXiv:2007.14381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14381)
- Solves 31 of 38 new tasks within 30 million candidates or 30 seconds; a DeepCoder-style premise-selection baseline (drop the 2 least likely operations) did not solve more tasks than the no-model baseline; the RobustFill baseline needed a beam of 80,000 and about 25 s per search on a 16 GB GPU. Most tasks have 2 to 4 I/O pairs — [BUSTLE, arXiv:2007.14381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14381)
- Time-limited comparison on 127 tasks (reported in the CrossBeam paper): baseline enumeration, Java, 3.4 s: 74; BUSTLE, Java, 3.4 s: 101; baseline, Java, 30 s: 91; BUSTLE, Java, 30 s: 112 — [CrossBeam, arXiv:2203.10452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10452)

#### DreamCoder (Ellis et al.; PLDI 2021)
- Text editing (SyGuS 2017, 108 problems): 3.7% solved within 10 minutes before learning, 79.6% after (average search 235 s -> 40 s); 84.3% under the CVC4 budget where CVC4 gets 82.4%. Trained on 128 generated tasks — [DreamCoder, arXiv:2006.08381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08381)
- Typical training "around a day" on 20-100 CPUs; the recursive-programming experiment about five days on 64 CPUs; rediscovering origami-style fold/unfold about a year of CPU time. The recognition model is trained on a 50/50 mix of replays and "fantasies" sampled from the library — [DreamCoder, arXiv:2006.08381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08381)
- A learned sorting routine uses 5 calls of a library that is four layers deep; written in the base language it has 32 calls and would take "in excess of 10^72 years" of brute-force search. Library depth correlates with tasks solved at r = 0.79. Per-domain solve rates and ablations are only in figures — [DreamCoder, arXiv:2006.08381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08381)

#### Distribution-based search: HeapSearch (Fijalkow et al.; AAAI 2022) and Bee Search (Ameen and Lelis; JAIR 2023)
- HeapSearch enumerates every program exactly once in non-increasing probability under a predicted PCFG ("loss optimal"); on 137 DreamCoder list tasks with 100 s and at most 1M programs it solves 97, A* 85, Threshold 83; throughput 38,735 programs/s versus 6,071 for A*. SQRT sampling is the optimal sampler, with expected tries (sum over x of sqrt(D(x)))^2 — [HeapSearch, arXiv:2110.12485](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.12485)
- Bee Search does best-first bottom-up search in a space of cost tuples so that it never builds a program more expensive than the solution and does not round costs; a new cost w_u(p) = 1 + sum(w_u(child) - log2 P(child)) lets BUSTLE's model have unbounded influence — [Bee Search, arXiv:2310.04327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04327)
- On 205 string tasks: Bee Search with w_u 184 (smaller DSL) and 132 (larger DSL with extra literals); BUSTLE 180 and 117; Brute with BUSTLE's cost 143 and 95. On the 38 BUSTLE tasks: Probe and Bee Search (Probe cost) 29, Bee Search (w_BUSTLE, w_u) 28, BUSTLE 27; with the larger DSL 15, 13 and 12.6. Runs used 2.4 GHz CPUs, 16 GB RAM and 120 minutes per task — [Bee Search, arXiv:2310.04327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04327)
- The authors conjecture that HeapSearch did poorly in their setting "because it is unable to perform observational equivalence"; "even BUS substantially outperforms both Brute and Heap Search" on the string benchmark — [Bee Search, arXiv:2310.04327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04327)

#### CrossBeam (Shi, Dai, Ellis, Sutton; ICLR 2022) and LambdaBeam (Shi, Dai, Li, Ellis, Sutton; NeurIPS 2023)
- CrossBeam: a model reads the set of values explored so far and chooses which to combine next; it is trained on-policy (beam-aware) on its own bottom-up searches, 10M string tasks, 8 V100 GPUs for at most 5 days — [CrossBeam, arXiv:2203.10452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10452)
- With a budget of 50,000 candidates: CrossBeam 95.4 of 127 (28.8 of 38 new, 66.6 of 89 SyGuS), BUSTLE 59 (15, 44). With 1M candidates: 104.6 vs 94. It needs "about 20x" fewer candidates than BUSTLE. Inductive logic programming: near 100% up to size 16 and over 50% up to size 19, while other methods drop to 0% by size 13; 28 of 30 handcrafted relations versus 18 for Metagol and 11-18 for Popper — [CrossBeam, arXiv:2203.10452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10452)
- LambdaBeam: about 13 million trainable parameters, trained on about 6.5 million tasks for "about a week" on 8 V100 GPUs; DSL of 23 first-order and 5 higher-order operations with freely composed lambdas; training programs have weight at most 12 — [LambdaBeam, arXiv:2306.02049](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02049)
- LambdaBeam with restarts solves 67.2 of 100 handwritten tasks (weights 3 to 19, median 9) in 10 minutes, 24% more than lambda^2; baselines include RobustFill with beam 65,536 and a 62B-parameter PaLM fine-tuned on Python. Restarting from scratch every 6 s helps because "it may be otherwise difficult to recover from exploring the wrong search direction". False positives were checked on 2 held-out test cases per task; the LLM's false positives often hard-code the examples — [LambdaBeam, arXiv:2306.02049](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02049)

#### ExeDec (Shi, Hong, Deng, Yin, Zaheer, Sutton; ICLR 2024)
- Five compositional generalization splits (length generalization, compose different concepts, switch concept order, compose new operation, add operation functionality); Transformers trained from scratch with 3 layers, embedding 512, hidden 1024, 4 heads, 500K steps of batch 128 — [ExeDec, arXiv:2307.13883](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13883)
- End-to-end accuracy with beam 10 (no generalization / average over the five generalization splits): RobustFill: ExeDec 95.5 / 87.0, no-subgoal ablation 94.9 / 80.2, plain Transformer 90.9 / 42.4, Latent Programmer 76.1 / 33.7. DeepCoder: ExeDec 61.2 / 23.1, ablation 64.7 / 18.1, Transformer 53.6 / 5.7, Latent Programmer 46.5 / 3.5 — [ExeDec, arXiv:2307.13883](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13883)
- The plain Transformer is "confused" by the shift in sub-program index between training and test lengths; step-by-step methods that re-read the execution state are far less sensitive. A few-shot LLM (PaLM 2) solves at most a handful of the 200 generalization problems per split — [ExeDec, arXiv:2307.13883](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13883)

#### Sketch-then-fill and other from-scratch neural/enumerative hybrids
- SketchAdapt (Nye, Hewitt, Tenenbaum, Solar-Lezama; ICML 2019): a seq2seq generator (about 7 million parameters) writes a sketch with holes and a DeepCoder-style recognizer (about 230,000 parameters) guides enumeration to fill them; the sketch target is chosen by a sampled time budget so the model learns how much to leave to enumeration — [SketchAdapt, arXiv:1902.06349](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.06349)
- SketchAdapt, list programs: trained on length 3, tested on length 4, the generator alone performs "very poorly"; about 24% of tasks were solved only by SketchAdapt versus about 2% only by the enumerator (under 10^4 candidates). String editing: 63.0% vs 57.2% for the generator alone. AlgoLisp with the concept "odd" held out of training: SketchAdapt 34.4% (even) / 29.8% (odd), enumerator only 23.7 / 0.0, generator only 4.5 / 1.1 — [SketchAdapt, arXiv:1902.06349](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.06349)
- Neural-guided deductive search (Kalyan/Vijayakumar, Mohta, Polozov, Batra, Jain, Gulwani; ICLR 2018): an LSTM scores grammar branches inside PROSE's top-down deductive search; trained on only 375 real tasks (about 400,000 search decisions). With a SINGLE example, accuracy on 73 test tasks: PROSE 67.12%, NGDS 68.49%, a DeepCoder-style model 35.81%, RobustFill 24.53%; speedup 1.67x geometric mean, up to 12x — [NGDS, arXiv:1804.01186](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01186)
- LaSynth (Chen, Song, Tian; NeurIPS 2021): restricted C programs with for-loops from 5 I/O pairs; a learned latent executor stands in for the missing partial-program interpreter: 55.2% generalization vs 38.6% without the executor, 37.6% RobustFill, 34.5% property signatures; retraining on its own (shorter) synthesized programs gives 56.0% then 56.5% — [LaSynth, arXiv:2107.00101](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.00101)
- Ouellette (2025): an encoder-decoder Transformer trained from scratch (4+4 layers, d_model 256, 200,000 examples) predicts the next DSL instruction from the tokenized execution states so far, inside a priority-queue tree search (3 minutes per task). On out-of-distribution compositions it scores 80% (median) versus 10% for the same network without search, 10% for test-time fine-tuning and 14.29% for AlphaEvolve; on ARC-AGI-2 training tasks it solves 83.33% of the at most 18 tasks its DSL can express — [Ouellette 2025, arXiv:2507.15877](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15877)
- Diffusion on syntax trees (Kapur, Jenner, Russell; 2024): a model trained to invert small random edits on syntax trees edits a program step by step while observing its rendered output, combined with search, for inverse graphics (abstract only read; no numbers) — [Diffusion on syntax trees, arXiv:2405.20519](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20519)

### Inferences
- Ranking of guidance types by measured benefit: operator-level priors (DeepCoder-style premise selection; TF-Coder's natural-language model; keyword gates belong here) < value-level scoring of intermediate results (BUSTLE, Bee Search) < value-level choice trained on-policy (CrossBeam, LambdaBeam) and execution-state policies with a value function (PCCoder, REPL). The evidence: premise selection added nothing in BUSTLE's domain and TF-Coder's language model added no task, while BUSTLE adds 27 tasks at 3.4 s and CrossBeam needs about 20x fewer candidates than BUSTLE.
- Length and composition generalization comes from re-deriving each decision from the executed state, not from model size. PCCoder (trained up to 12, 60% at 14), REPL (trained to 30 actions, solves 40+), ExeDec (42.4 -> 87.0) and Ouellette 2025 (10% -> 80% with search) all make short-horizon decisions on concrete values. A postfix coder that sees the value of its partial program is already in this family; what the literature adds on top is (1) a value head, (2) on-policy training on the search's own states, (3) restarts or particle resampling instead of a fixed-width beam, and (4) predicting an intermediate value (subgoal) before the code that reaches it.
- Model size is not the binding limit: LambdaBeam's guide has about 13M parameters, SketchAdapt's generator about 7M, BUSTLE's a small MLP trained on a CPU. A 12.4M-parameter coder is in the same class as the largest of these.
- A right program that sits a median 36 nats below the beam's best cannot be reached by widening the beam or by sampling: any search that visits programs in probability order (the best case, per HeapSearch) may need on the order of e^36 (about 4x10^15) candidates. The gap must be closed by changing the distribution (training on the states and programs that are actually needed) or by changing the factorization of the search (bottom-up over values with a context-free cost, where a locally improbable token no longer vetoes the whole program).
- Published time budgets are 1 to 3 orders of magnitude above "a few seconds": 30 s (BUSTLE, CrossBeam), 2 min (REPL), 5 min (TF-Coder), 10 min (Probe, LambdaBeam, DreamCoder), 120 min (Bee Search). The only directly comparable point is BUSTLE at 3.4 s in Java (101 vs 74 of 127); since the unguided baseline reaches only 91 at 30 s, a cheap value-level guide is worth more than a 9x larger time budget in that domain.

### Gaps
- Parameter counts are not reported for DeepCoder, RobustFill, PCCoder, REPL, BUSTLE, CrossBeam and ExeDec; only layer sizes are.
- RobustFill's accuracy with a single observed example, DreamCoder's per-domain solve rates, and several BUSTLE/CrossBeam/LambdaBeam baseline numbers exist only in figures and could not be quoted.
- No 2026 paper was found that proposes a new from-scratch neural-guided bottom-up synthesizer benchmarked against CrossBeam or LambdaBeam (one search, so absence is weak evidence).
- No paper was found that studies a from-scratch model of about 10M parameters on MBPP-like breadth (one sentence plus one example over hundreds of library operators); every system above works in a narrow DSL of 20 to 134 operations.
- The full text of Chen, Liu and Song (2019) was read from a text mirror of the ICLR paper because OpenReview blocked automated access; the table's row labels were partly lost in extraction, so the mapping of numbers to rows should be rechecked against the PDF before quoting more than the headline 92.00%.

## 2. Using natural language together with examples to prune and rank a large enumerative space

### Takeaway
When examples are few, the language channel carries most of the information: examples-only synthesizers recover the intended program on a few percent up to about 40% of tasks (56% on one small CSS benchmark) where language plus examples reaches 60-90%, and adding examples to a language-only model is worth anywhere from 8 to more than 35 further points, mainly as a filter. The size of the gain depends on how the language is used: as a structured prior over sketches, whole programs or components it is large; as bag-of-words operator weights it is close to zero.

### Cited Findings

#### Regular expressions from language plus examples
- REGEL (Chen, Wang, Ye, Durrett, Dillig; PLDI 2020): a semantic parser turns the sentence into ranked hierarchical sketches and a PBE engine completes each sketch against the examples with over/under-approximation pruning (up to 25 sketches in parallel). Overall "Regel achieves 80% accuracy whereas the NLP-only and PBE-only baselines achieve 43% and 26%" — [REGEL, arXiv:1908.03316](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.03316)
- REGEL per benchmark: DeepRegex set (200 tasks, about 4 positive and 5 negative examples, 10 s): REGEL 151 (75.5%), language-only DeepRegex 134 (67%), examples-only at most 66 (33%); with further example rounds REGEL reaches 185 (92.5%). StackOverflow set (122 tasks, 60 s, top 5): REGEL up to 74 (60.7%), examples-only 18 (14.7%), language-only 3 (2.4%) — [REGEL, arXiv:1908.03316](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.03316)
- OpSynth (Ye, Chen, Dillig, Durrett; ICLR 2021): a small tree decoder conditioned on the sentence (Bi-LSTM, hidden size 100; 3,520 training examples) scores partial syntax trees; best-first search with an admissible upper bound returns the highest-probability program that satisfies the examples, pruning partial programs that provably cannot — [OpSynth, arXiv:2010.01678](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01678)
- OpSynth on StructuredRegex Test (solved / consistent with examples): OpSynth 60.8% / 88.4% (880 states, 9.5 s); beam search with the same model plus pruning 58.0% / 87.8%; OpSynth without pruning 56.6% / 78.5%. Examples-only baselines: AlphaRegex 3.6% / 51.8%, RobustFill 3.5% / 39.4%, DeepCoder 1.1% / 6.2% — [OpSynth, arXiv:2010.01678](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01678)
- OpSynth versus beam width on Test-E (solved): beam 5 39.0%, beam 10 42.8%, beam 15 44.1%, beam 20 45.8% (1,430 states) versus exact best-first 48.9% (1,320 states). Among example-consistent programs, about 62% of the model-optimal ones are equivalent to the target regex, against about 30% of the nearly-optimal ones — [OpSynth, arXiv:2010.01678](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01678)
- Multi-modal program inference, NLX (Rahmani, Raza, Gulwani, Le, Morris, Radhakrishna, Soares, Tiwari; OOPSLA 2021): GPT-3 writes candidate programs from the sentence; their components and operator frequencies seed and rank a component-based search against the examples. DeepRegex: NLX and REGEL 90% each, examples-only breadth-first search 41%, GPT-3 alone 18%. StackOverflow: NLX 70%, REGEL 25%, search 10%, GPT-3 5%. CSS selectors: NLX 75%, brute force 56%, GPT-3 20% — [NLX, arXiv:2109.02445](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.02445)
- NLX ablation (relative to the full system): seeding the search with all atomic components instead of the model's components is 40% lower; random ranking instead of model-based ranking 10% lower; full-DSL expansion 5% lower; a fixed prompt 14% lower. The ground-truth share among all model-proposed components is under 0.06 and rises about 8x when only the frequent ones are kept — [NLX, arXiv:2109.02445](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.02445)

#### Data-frame and tensor code
- AutoPandas (Bavishi, Lemieux, Fox, Sen, Stoica; OOPSLA 2019): generators for 119 pandas functions whose choice points are decided by graph neural networks reading the I/O data frames; solves 17 of 26 real benchmarks (snippet-level; examples only, no language) — [AutoPandas (NSF public copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10195539)
- Jigsaw (Jain, Vaidyanath, Iyer, Natarajan, Parthasarathy, Rajamani, Sharma; ICSE 2022): the language model sees only the sentence; the I/O example is used afterwards to check candidates and to drive repairs (variable renaming, AutoPandas-style argument search, learned AST rewrites). PandasEval1: Codex 52.0% -> 66.7%; PandasEval2: Codex 31.2% -> 72.2%, GPT-3 6.6% -> 35.1% — [Jigsaw, arXiv:2112.02969](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02969)
- Jigsaw versus examples-only synthesis on the 27 tasks AutoPandas supports: AutoPandas 16, Codex 20, Jigsaw 23; the authors note that 16 of these 27 tasks are under-specified by the I/O examples alone. The paper does not report how often a candidate passes the I/O check and is still wrong — [Jigsaw, arXiv:2112.02969](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02969)
- TF-Coder's natural-language model (TF-IDF or naive Bayes over docstrings and GitHub snippets, multiplying the weight of predicted operators by 0.75) solved no additional task (61 of 70, same as no model) and gave 1.08x-1.19x total speedup; the model that reads the example tensors solved 2 more tasks and gave 1.46x — [TF-Coder, arXiv:2003.09040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.09040)

#### Sketch generation then enumeration, and description plus examples with small from-scratch models
- AlgoLisp (Polosukhin and Skidanov; 2018): 79,214 training tasks with a description and tests; average program depth about 8 and length about 25 tokens; model hidden size 100. Test accuracy: description-only Seq2Tree 61.0%; Seq2Tree plus tree search that returns the first program passing the example tests 85.8%; attentional seq2seq 54.1% -> 72.3% with search; examples-only model 2.2% -> 12.8% with search; search without a model 0.6% — [Neural Program Search, arXiv:1802.04335](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04335)
- SketchAdapt on AlgoLisp (test): 90.0% with description and examples, 88.6% for the generator alone, 7.3% for the enumerator alone, 8.3% for SketchAdapt given examples only. On string editing the sketch-plus-enumeration system solves 63.0% versus 57.2% for the generator alone — [SketchAdapt, arXiv:1902.06349](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.06349)
- Synchromesh (Poesia, Polozov, Le, Tiwari, Soares, Meek, Gulwani; ICLR 2022): constrained semantic decoding plus similarity-tuned example retrieval raises validity (GPT-3 13B on SQL: 43% -> 72%; Vega-Lite 55% -> 100%) and accuracy (Codex SQL execution accuracy 56% -> 64%; SMCalFlow 45% -> 63%), but the authors state that conceptual errors remain and constraints are "usually insufficient to correct the prediction" in those cases — [Synchromesh, arXiv:2201.11227](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11227)

#### Language used at training time (LAPS; Wong, Ellis, Tenenbaum, Andreas; ICML 2021)
- Percent of held-out tasks solved (Strings / Graphics best run / Scenes curriculum): DreamCoder without language 33.4 / 49.55 / 67.80; a multimodal baseline that just feeds language to the search network 46.00 / 26.12 / 76.50; LAPS in neural search 52.20 / 92.79 / 95.6; LAPS with mutual exclusivity and language-guided compression 54.60 / 98.19 / 95.6 — [LAPS, arXiv:2106.11053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.11053)
- With NO language at test time: enumeration with DreamCoder's best library 27.2 / 41.44 / 53.6; enumeration with the LAPS library 33.2 / 62.16 / 93.04; example-only neural synthesis with the LAPS library 52.4 / 91.0 / 95.6. Models trained on synthetic template language still work with human language at test (54.60 / 89.20 / 97.4) — [LAPS, arXiv:2106.11053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.11053)
- The authors observe that "not all shared subroutines encountered in training generalize well to new tasks. Adding poor abstractions can actually be detrimental: they increase the combinatorial search space" — [LAPS, arXiv:2106.11053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.11053)

#### Large language models with a few tests (the MBPP setting)
- MBPP few-shot with a 137B-parameter model: 43.2% of problems solved when no test case is shown, 55.2% with one, 59.0% with two, 58.4% with three (80 samples per problem). Of 50 problems re-checked with adversarial tests, 33 had a solution passing the three given tests and 29 still passed the new tests, i.e. about 12% of "solved" problems were false positives — [Austin et al. 2021, arXiv:2108.07732](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.07732)
- MBR-EXEC (Shi, Fried, Ghazvininejad, Zettlemoyer, Wang; 2022): pick the sample whose outputs agree most with the other samples on a test input, with no expected output needed; MBPP 47.3% (greedy) -> 58.2% with 25 samples and one test input; Spider 50.8% -> 63.6%; NL2Bash 52.8% -> 58.5%. Several test inputs beat one — [MBR-EXEC, arXiv:2204.11454](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.11454)
- CodeT (Chen et al.; 2022): the model also writes tests; programs and tests are grouped into consensus sets scored by (number of programs) x (number of tests). HumanEval pass@1 47.0% -> 65.8% and MBPP 58.1% -> 67.7% for code-davinci-002; clustering by outputs alone (AlphaCode-style) gives 55.1% and 62.0% because trivial programs cluster together. For the weaker code-cushman-001, 53 of 164 problems had a correct sample that was not ranked first — [CodeT, arXiv:2207.10397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10397)
- LEVER (Ni et al.; ICML 2023): a trained verifier reads the sentence, the program and its execution result, and its score is combined with the generation probability while summing over programs with identical execution results; +4.6% to +10.9% over code-davinci-002 on four datasets — [LEVER, arXiv:2302.08468](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08468)
- Coder-Reviewer reranking (Zhang et al.; 2022): multiply p(program | sentence) by p(sentence | program); up to 17% absolute gain over reranking with the coder alone across six datasets and eight models (abstract only read) — [Coder-Reviewer, arXiv:2211.16490](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.16490)
- "PBE Meets LLM" (Zhang and Park; 2025): on table-transformation tasks with 1 to 5 example records, classical PBE (Foofah) 0.571, GPT-4o one-shot 0.797, and a hybrid that runs the PBE solver first and falls back to the LLM 0.863 — [PBE Meets LLM, arXiv:2507.05403](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05403)

#### Ranking among example-consistent programs when there is one example and no language
- Ellis and Gulwani (IJCAI 2017): 447 string-transformation and 488 text-extraction problems, almost all with ONE example (all extraction problems; 91% of string problems). Picking a random member of the size-1000 frontier of consistent programs is right 13.7% (strings) and 14.7% (extraction) of the time. PROSE's hand-tuned ranking: 76.4% and 65.8%. A learned ranker on features of the outputs on unlabeled inputs, of the execution trace and of the program: 83.5% +/- 3% and 69.2% +/- 2% (test, 10-fold). Output features alone 66.5% / 68.2%; program-structure features alone 57.9% / 49.9% — [Ellis and Gulwani 2017, IJCAI](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2017/227)
- Same paper: "Program outputs provide a surprisingly strong signal ... predicting based on outputs is less prone to over fitting"; about 1 second of descriptor computation gives most of the benefit; enumerating the top 1000 programs costs about 2.9 s versus 0.45 s for the top 1 — [Ellis and Gulwani 2017, IJCAI](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2017/227)
- BlinkFill (Singh; VLDB 2016) uses the structure of all (unlabeled) input rows to constrain the program space: 1.27 versus 1.53 examples needed on average over 207 benchmarks and 41x faster than FlashFill on average (median 11x) (snippet-level) — [BlinkFill, Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/blinkfill-semi-supervised-programming-by-example-for-syntactic-string-transformations/)
- Pragmatic synthesis (Pu, Ellis, Kryven, Tenenbaum, Solar-Lezama; NeurIPS 2020): rank programs by how likely a cooperative user would have chosen these examples to communicate each program (Rational Speech Acts) instead of treating all consistent programs alike; in a user study (N = 48) people needed on average 2.8 fewer symbols with the pragmatic synthesizer and 77% found it easier — [Pragmatic synthesis, arXiv:2007.05060](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.05060)

### Inferences
- Keyword gates that open operators are the weak way to use language: it is the same mechanism as TF-Coder's language model (no extra task solved) and DeepCoder-style premise selection (no gain in BUSTLE's domain). The strong ways all let a language-conditioned model propose structure: sketches (REGEL: 26% -> 80%), a full distribution over programs searched exactly (OpSynth: 3.6% -> 60.8%), or components harvested from model samples (NLX: seeding from model components is worth 40% relative).
- Examples-only search finds an example-consistent program far more often than the intended one (AlphaRegex: consistent on 51.8% of tasks, intended on 3.6%). The gap between those two numbers is the coincidence rate, and it is the language-conditioned probability that closes it: among consistent programs the model's top choice is right about 62% of the time versus about 30% for runners-up.
- The one-example regime the target system is in has a published reference point: a random member of the consistent frontier is right about 14% of the time, and a learned ranker that looks at the outputs on other inputs lifts this to 69-84%. About 70 consistent candidates per task with the right one "rarely preferred" corresponds to the "random baseline" row, which suggests the ranking signal, not the candidate pool, is what is missing.
- Exact best-first search under the model beat wider beams at equal cost (48.9% vs 45.8% at about 1,300-1,400 states). For a small coder this argues for a priority-queue search over partial programs with example-based pruning rather than a fixed-width beam.
- One example already captures most of the benefit of examples for a language-conditioned generator (MBPP: 43.2% -> 55.2% with one test, 58.4% with three), so the low ceiling is not caused by having only one example as such; it is caused by the search not proposing the right program and the ranker not preferring it.
- Training with language improves the search heuristic and the library even when language is absent at test time (LAPS), and synthetic template language transfers to human language. This supports generating descriptions for synthetic or relabeled programs rather than training an example-only pathway.

### Gaps
- Singh and Gulwani (CAV 2015, "Predicting a correct program in programming by example") could not be read (PDF text extraction failed); its one-example ranking numbers are not included.
- AutoPandas numbers come from a search summary only; the per-benchmark comparison was not read.
- LAPS, CodeT, LEVER, Coder-Reviewer and MBR-EXEC all use either several examples, or large pretrained models, or both; no study was found that measures the language channel for a from-scratch model of about 10M parameters with exactly one example.
- Jigsaw and AlgoLisp do not report the rate of programs that pass the shown examples but fail held-out ones.

## 3. LLM-guided enumeration and its lessons for tiny guides

### Takeaway
A guide that almost never writes the right program can still tell an enumerator which components to use: fitting a per-task context-free grammar to a handful of model samples raised solve rates from 40% (unguided) and 6% (model alone) to 58%, and the union of model and enumerator beat either alone by 27-30 points. The other durable lessons are feedback (update the guide from partial progress), bandit-style allocation over candidates to refine, and self-training on the search's own relabeled attempts.

### Cited Findings
- HySynth (Barke, Gonzalez, Kasibatla, Berg-Kirkpatrick, Polikarpova; NeurIPS 2024): sample N programs from an LLM for the task (N = 100 by default), fit a PCFG by maximum likelihood with add-alpha smoothing (alpha "typically set to 1"), convert rule probabilities to costs (-log p), and run bottom-up enumeration in cost order with observational equivalence. Over 299 tasks (160 ARC object tasks, 70 string, 69 tensor): HySynth 58%, unguided search 40%, LLM alone 6% — [HySynth, arXiv:2405.15880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15880)
- HySynth details: on the tensor domain it solves 96% although the LLM alone solves none; on ARC 58 tasks versus 51 for the ARGA baseline reproduced at the same 10-minute limit. With N = 10, 20, 50 or 100 samples the best and worst differ by only 2 problems on ARC and tensor, and by 9 on strings. Even invalid completions are usable (only 37.5% of GPT-4o string completions parse; operator counts are taken from the token stream) — [HySynth, arXiv:2405.15880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15880)
- HySynth's stated limitation: guidance is only as good as the completions; if they contain many irrelevant operators the guided search can be slower than the unguided one. The authors argue a context-free surrogate is adequate because, for one fixed task, what matters is which components are likely, and a context-free cost is what bottom-up search can exploit — [HySynth, arXiv:2405.15880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15880)
- Guiding enumerative synthesis with LLMs (Li, Parsert, Polgreen; CAV 2024), 609 SyGuS problems, 600 s, GPT-3.5: plain enumerator 188.7 (31.0%); LLM alone 303 (49.8%); A* with uniform weights 309 (50.7%); A* with an LLM-derived pCFG 322 (52.9%); union of LLM and pCFG-guided A* 488 (80.1%); enumerator that calls the LLM during search with partial progress and counterexamples (iLLM-synth) 408 (67.0%); cvc5 415 (68.1%) — [Li, Parsert, Polgreen 2024, arXiv:2403.03997](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03997)
- "Is programming by example solved by LLMs?" (Li and Ellis; NeurIPS 2024): DeepSeekCoder 7B / 33B fine-tuned on 10k-32k synthetic problems generated from 40-200 seed problems; text editing 82% generalization accuracy (88% of problems have some consistent sample) versus 33% for FlashFill; LOGO graphics 90%; "tens or hundreds" of samples per problem, each checked against the examples — [Li and Ellis 2024, arXiv:2406.08316](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08316)
- Li and Ellis, out of distribution: a 7B model scores 76% on PROSE when seeded with PROSE-like problems but 59% when seeded with SyGuS. An adaptation loop (sample programs for unlabeled target problems, add any program that passes its examples back to the seed set, regenerate data, retrain) recovers about 10 absolute points or more in each domain and roughly triples solved LOGO problems. The compute needed is predicted better by the description length under the fine-tuned posterior than by program size or prior likelihood, so the model "is not simply doing blind guess-and-check" — [Li and Ellis 2024, arXiv:2406.08316](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08316)
- REx (Tang, Hu, Zhou, Zhong, Zheng, Si, Ellis; NeurIPS 2024): choosing which candidate program to refine next is an arm-acquiring bandit solved by Thompson sampling from Beta(1 + C*h, 1 + C*(1-h) + N), where h is the fraction of the specification satisfied and N the number of failed refinements of that program; 1.5x-5x fewer LLM calls than greedy, breadth-first or fixed-width refinement; 28 of 38 nonlinear loop invariants versus 24 for the best prior tool; the gain in final solve rate at large budgets is modest — [REx, arXiv:2405.17503](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17503)
- Execution-guided within-prompt search (Verbruggen, Tiwari, Singh, Le, Gulwani; ICLR 2025): the LLM proposes straight-line code one line at a time; each line is executed on the examples and its values are written back into the prompt, so the model acts as policy and implicit value function and can backtrack; five benchmarks (abstract only read; no numbers) — [Verbruggen et al., ICLR 2025](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/poster/29749)
- SOAR (Pourcel, Colas, Oudeyer; ICML 2025): 3k samples + 3k refinements per ARC task, then every sampled program is relabeled as the correct solution of the synthetic task defined by its own outputs (hindsight relabeling), and the model is fine-tuned for sampling and for refinement; four rounds on ARC-train and two test-time rounds on ARC-test without labels. ARC-AGI public test, 7B model: 1.00% one-shot, 5.63% with 6k samples, 14.25% with sample-and-refine, 36.25% after self-improvement; 32B: 12.88% -> 25.25% -> 44.38%; ensemble of all models 52.00% (oracle 57.25%). Search without learning saturates after about 5.2k attempts — [SOAR, arXiv:2507.14172](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14172)
- SOAR ablations: training on correct programs only 34.67, uniform relabeled data 32.38, greedy-diverse selection of relabeled data 36.46 (no fine-tuning 29.29). Final answers are chosen by weighted majority vote over programs' outputs (weight = count + 1000 x train accuracy); the vote is on average 9.5 points below the oracle — [SOAR, arXiv:2507.14172](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14172)
- Genetic programming versus LLMs on PSB2 (Hernandez, Saini, Ketron, Moore; 2025): PushGP (examples only) and GPT-4o (text, examples, or both) each solve tasks the other cannot; combined they solve 23 of 25; PushGP and examples-only prompting degrade as the number of examples shrinks while text prompts do not (abstract only read) — [GP and LLMs, arXiv:2508.03966](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03966)
- Guided Tensor Lifting (PLDI 2025) is a further LLM-guided enumerative synthesizer in this line (snippet-level; not read) — [Guided Tensor Lifting, arXiv:2504.19705](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19705)

### Inferences
- The HySynth result is the most directly transferable idea for a tiny guide. The 12.4M coder plays the LLM's role; drawing 10-100 programs from it costs milliseconds to a second on CPU, against 4-20 s for 100 GPT-4o samples in the paper. The operator counts of those samples, smoothed, replace keyword gates as the enumerator's cost function. The right whole program need not be in the samples; only its operators need to be over-represented relative to a uniform prior over about 780 operators.
- Whether this works for the target system is an empirical question that can be answered offline before building anything: on tasks where a teacher solution exists, measure how many of the solution's operators appear among the coder's samples (component recall) and what rank they get. HySynth's own warning (irrelevant operators make guided search slower than unguided) is the failure mode to look for.
- Unions beat single methods because the failure sets differ (Li et al.: 49.8% and 52.9% alone, 80.1% together; PBE-first hybrids; PushGP plus GPT-4o). For the target system this argues for keeping engine and coder as separate routes and, importantly for coincidence control, treating agreement between routes as evidence (see section 6).
- Feedback during search matters more than a better static prior: iLLM-synth 67.0% versus 52.9% for the static pCFG; Probe's just-in-time update 25 versus 9 on the larger grammar. With one example there are no "partially satisfied example sets", so the feedback signal has to be a graded distance between a candidate's output and the target output (type match, length, shared elements, edit distance), which is an adaptation not tested in these papers.
- The posterior description length under the tuned model predicting search cost (Li and Ellis) is the same phenomenon as "36 nats below the beam": out-of-distribution problems are exactly those with long posterior description length, and both Li and Ellis and SOAR fix this by retraining on the model's own verified or relabeled attempts on the target distribution, not by searching longer.

### Gaps
- No paper was found in which the guide is a small from-scratch model rather than an LLM; the transfer of HySynth-style surrogates to a 10M-parameter guide is untested in the literature.
- HySynth's per-domain numbers for Probe, TF-Coder and ARGA baselines, and its results with GPT-3.5 and DeepSeek, are only in figures.
- 2026 follow-ups specific to LLM-guided enumeration for programming by example were not found in two searches; the 2025-2026 items listed above are the ones located.

## 4. ARC-AGI lessons for small from-scratch models (2024-2026)

### Takeaway
On ARC the ingredients with the largest measured effect are per-task test-time adaptation, heavy rule-preserving augmentation, scoring or voting each candidate under many augmented views, and ensembling program induction with direct output prediction; the special architectures (hierarchy, deep recursion) contribute little once those are controlled for. For a language-conditioned synthesizer with a single example, the parts that transfer are view-based scoring for selection, induction-first ensembles, and self-training on relabeled search attempts; leave-one-out test-time training and puzzle-identity embeddings do not transfer because they need several demonstrations of the test task.

Example at the application layer: a refinement harness raised Gemini 3 Pro from 31% ($0.81 per task) to 54% (about $30 per task) on ARC-AGI-2 — [ARC Prize 2025 report, arXiv:2601.10904](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10904)
- The 2025 report states that frontier reasoning performance "remains fundamentally constrained to knowledge coverage" and that overfitting to ARC-AGI-1 and ARC-AGI-2 "is now occurring". The report and the blog disagree on MindsAI's score (12.64% in the table, 15.42% in an interview section) — [ARC Prize 2025 results blog](https://arcprize.org/blog/arc-prize-2025-results-analysis)

#### Induction versus transduction (Li et al.; 2024, "BARC")
- Same 8B model fine-tuned either to write Python (induction) or to output the grid directly (transduction) on 400k synthetic problems made by LLM remixes of 100-160 hand-written seed programs. Public evaluation, two tries: induction with 20k sampled programs 38.00%; transduction 29.125% plain, 35.25% with reranking, 39.25% with test-time training, 43.00% with both; ensemble 56.75% — [BARC, arXiv:2411.02272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02272)
- The ensemble is induction first: if any sampled program fits all training pairs, use it; otherwise use the transduction output. The two solve largely different problems (induction: precise computation and composition; transduction: fuzzier perceptual concepts) even with identical training problems and architecture — [BARC, arXiv:2411.02272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02272)
- "About 9% of samples that fit the training examples are false-positives"; in about half of the affected problems the false-positive share is under 0.5, so majority voting over outputs removes many. Performance grows with synthetic data volume and with samples at test time but saturates with more hand-written seeds. A scaled-down version scores 19% on the private set (induction with 384 samples: 4%) — [BARC, arXiv:2411.02272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02272)

#### Test-time training (Akyürek et al.; 2024)
- For each test task, build training tasks by holding out one demonstration at a time (leave-one-out), apply invertible transformations (rotations, flips, transpose, translations, scaling, color and order shuffles), cap at 250 examples, and train a per-task LoRA (rank 128, 2 epochs). About 12 hours for 100 tasks on one A100 — [Test-time training, arXiv:2411.07279](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07279)
- Results: a fine-tuned 1B model goes from 5% to 29% on an 80-task subset ("approximately 6x"); on the full public evaluation set 18.3% -> 47.1%; with the BARC transduction model 53.0%; ensembled with BARC's program synthesis 61.9% (average human 60.2%); 47.5% on the semi-private set. Ablations on the 80-task subset: no transformations costs 16 tasks, a shared instead of per-task adapter costs 7, training on direct I/O pairs instead of in-context tasks costs 11 (the paper's task counts and percentages do not reconcile exactly) — [Test-time training, arXiv:2411.07279](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07279)

#### Latent Program Network (Bonnet and Macfarlane; 2024)
- An encoder maps each I/O pair to a Gaussian over a latent "program" vector; a decoder produces the output from a new input and the latent; at test time the latent is refined by gradient ascent on the likelihood of the given pairs, starting from the encoder's guess; training includes 0-5 such gradient steps in the loop — [LPN, arXiv:2411.08706](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08706)
- Pattern task (973k-parameter model): 8.6% with no test-time search, 44.6% with 1 gradient step, 85.4% with 5, 98.4% with 20, 99.5% with 100; a model trained without gradient steps in the loop reaches only 67.5% at 100. Out-of-distribution pattern task: 0.0% without search, 88.0% with 100 steps, versus 0.3% for parameter fine-tuning. String manipulation: in-context learning 77.7%, LPN 71.3% -> 86.1% with 10 steps — [LPN, arXiv:2411.08706](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08706)
- ARC-AGI (178M parameters, trained from scratch on about 51M re-arc pairs): evaluation set 7.75% at the smallest compute budget -> 15.50% with latent search; encoder initialization is required (search from a Gaussian start reaches only 10.75%); in-distribution 68.75% -> 80.00% — [LPN, arXiv:2411.08706](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08706)

#### CodeIt (Butt et al.; ICML 2024)
- A 220M CodeT5+ policy samples 24 programs per task per iteration over a hand-written ARC DSL; every syntactically valid program is stored with the output it actually produced (hindsight relabeling) and replayed with priority proportional to how many demonstration outputs it matches. 59/400 evaluation tasks (14.75%) — [CodeIt, arXiv:2402.04858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04858)
- Ablations (cumulative solved of 400): full 59; no relabeling 42; no prioritized sampling 58; no pretraining (from scratch) 35; policy conditioned on ONE demonstration pair instead of all 51; no mutation of training programs 20. Search-only baselines that just mutate training programs solve 42 and 38, and random sampling 6, so the from-scratch variant (35) stayed below the mutation baseline within 100 meta-iterations. Without pretraining, generalization between tasks starts only after about 50 meta-iterations — [CodeIt, arXiv:2402.04858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04858)

#### Hierarchical Reasoning Model and the ARC Prize analysis
- HRM (Wang et al.; 2025): 27M parameters, "using only 1000 training samples" per the abstract, no pretraining or chain-of-thought data — [HRM, arXiv:2506.21734](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.21734)
- ARC Prize verification: 32% on the ARC-AGI-1 semi-private set ($1.48 per task, 9h16m) against a claimed 41% on public evaluation; 2% on ARC-AGI-2 — [ARC Prize HRM analysis](https://arcprize.org/blog/hrm-analysis)
- ARC Prize ablations: a plain Transformer of the same size comes within about 5 points with no tuning; the outer refinement loop is the main driver (+13 points from 1 to 2 loops; 1 to 8 loops doubles the score; training with 16 loops and inferring with 1 beats training and inferring with 1 by more than 15 points); adaptive halting adds a few points; about 300 augmentations reach near-maximum and 30 come within 4%; training-time augmentation matters more than inference-time augmentation — [ARC Prize HRM analysis](https://arcprize.org/blog/hrm-analysis)
- Training only on the 400 evaluation tasks (no training set, no ConceptARC) still gives 31% versus 41%, so most performance comes from training on the demonstration pairs of the tasks being evaluated. Each task is fed as an input grid plus a learned puzzle_id embedding with no few-shot examples, so the model "can only handle puzzle IDs it saw during training", which ARC Prize calls "a major limitation" — [ARC Prize HRM analysis](https://arcprize.org/blog/hrm-analysis)

#### Tiny Recursive Model
- TRM (Jolicoeur-Martineau; 2025): one 2-layer network (7M parameters with attention, 5M as an MLP for Sudoku) that repeatedly updates a latent z (n = 6 times) and then the answer y, for T = 3 cycles, with deep supervision over up to 16 steps and EMA 0.999. Sudoku-Extreme 87.4% (HRM 55.0%), Maze-Hard 85.3% (74.5%), ARC-AGI-1 44.6% (40.3%), ARC-AGI-2 7.8% (5.0%). A 27M direct-prediction baseline scores 21.0% on ARC-AGI-1 — [TRM, arXiv:2510.04871](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04871)
- TRM ablations on Sudoku-Extreme (from 87.4%): 4 layers instead of 2: 79.5%; no EMA 79.9%; two separate networks 82.4%; fewer recursions (T=2, n=2) 73.7%; one-step gradient approximation 56.5%; self-attention instead of MLP 74.7%. Training: 1K Sudoku examples with 1,000 augmentations each; ARC: 1,000 augmentations, about 3 days on 4 H100. The author "suspect[s] it has to do with overfitting" but offers no theory — [TRM, arXiv:2510.04871](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04871)
- Independent analysis of the ARC Prize TRM checkpoint (ARC-AGI-1 public evaluation): 40.00% with 1,000 augmentations and majority voting versus 29.25% with a single pass; replacing the puzzle identity token by a blank or a random one gives 0.00%; recursion step 1 already gives 38.25% and step 4 gives 40.50%. The authors conclude performance comes from "an interaction between efficiency, task-specific conditioning, and aggressive test-time compute, rather than from arbitrarily deep internal reasoning dynamics" — [TRM on ARC-AGI-1 analysis, arXiv:2512.11847](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.11847)
- A TRM pre-trained on public ARC tasks and then fine-tuned on the competition tasks fits the competition's compute limit, whereas training from scratch on them does not (snippet-level) — [Test-time adaptation of TRM, arXiv:2511.02886](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02886)

#### CompressARC (Liao and Gu; 2025)
- No training set at all: for each puzzle a 76K-parameter equivariant network is trained from random initialization for 2,000 steps (about 20 minutes) to minimize a description-length objective (KL of a latent code plus reconstruction cross-entropy) over the puzzle with the answer left blank; 20% on the ARC-AGI-1 evaluation set and 34.75% on the training set; a U-Net trained the same way gets 0.75% — [CompressARC, arXiv:2512.06104](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06104)
- The authors' argument: by Occam's razor the shortest program that reproduces the puzzle should fill in the unknown grid consistently with the rule ("The MDL endows CompressARC with extreme generalization abilities typically unheard of in deep learning"). It extends patterns over short but not long ranges — [CompressARC, arXiv:2512.06104](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06104)

#### VARC: "ARC is a vision problem" (Hu et al.; 2025)
- A plain ViT trained from scratch on the 400 ARC-1 training tasks plus 1,000 RE-ARC pairs per task (about 400k pairs), then trained per test task for 100 epochs on 51 augmented copies; 18M parameters: 54.5% on ARC-1 and 8.3% on ARC-2; ensemble with a 55M U-Net 60.4% and 11.1% — [VARC, arXiv:2511.14761](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14761)
- Ablations (ARC-1): test-time training alone without offline training 26.4%; offline tasks 16 -> 43.1%, 80 -> 49.6%, 400 -> 54.5%; RE-ARC pairs per task 0 -> 31.5%, 10 -> 38.6%, 100 -> 52.3%, 1,000 -> 54.0%; model size 6M 44.4%, 18M 54.5%, 66M 53.0% (authors attribute the drop to overfitting); single view 35.9% versus 510-view majority voting 49.8% (first guess); scale augmentation +6.2, translation augmentation +2.9, 2D instead of 1D positions +3.5; independent per-task adaptation beats joint adaptation by about 10 points — [VARC, arXiv:2511.14761](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14761)

#### The ARChitects: product of experts over augmented views (Franzen, Disselhoff, Hartmann; 2025)
- Candidates are generated by depth-first search over token probabilities, keeping every completion whose probability exceeds a threshold; each candidate is then re-scored under every augmented view of the task and the scores are multiplied. Public evaluation with an 8B model: baseline 18.3%; + per-task test-time training 44.5%; + 16 augmentations 62.5%; + product-of-views scoring 67.6%; + DFS candidate generation 71.6% (about 2 cents per task). With a 3B model: 14.9 -> 40.9 -> 52.9 -> 59.5 -> 61.4% — [Product of Experts, arXiv:2505.07859](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.07859)
- Recall versus selection: threshold 20%: 4.9 candidates per task, correct answer among them for 73.5% of tasks, selected for 70.0%; threshold 9%: 9.3 candidates, 76.0% recall, 71.6% selected; threshold 0.5%: 84.7 candidates, 83.5% recall, 71.8% selected — [Product of Experts, arXiv:2505.07859](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.07859)

#### 2026 items (lower confidence)
- A Cognizant AI Lab blog post dated 21 September 2026 reports a 75M-parameter autoregressive Transformer trained from scratch only on ARC-related data (including the demonstration pairs of the public evaluation tasks): about 76% (two guesses) on the ARC-AGI-1 public evaluation set, about 80% for an eight-model ensemble, about 17% / 22% on ARC-AGI-2; credited ingredients are 2D position coordinates, rule-preserving augmentation, predicting four tokens ahead (70% -> 76%), and reranking candidates by symbolic checks and augmented likelihood. Self-reported, public sets only, no paper — [Cognizant AI Lab blog](https://www.cognizant.com/us/en/ai-lab/blog/efficient-ai-reasoning-arc-agi)
- "Compositional Neuro-Symbolic Reasoning" (Das, Ghugarkar, Bhat, Aali; April 2026): symbolic scene abstraction, LLM-proposed patterns from a fixed 22-pattern DSL, filtering by consistency across all demonstrations with a preference for the shortest composition, then LLM generation with cell-wise voting; 24.4% on ARC-AGI-2 public evaluation versus about 16% for the base LLM; ablation: hints only 20.5%, voting only 17.5%, neither 15.0%. Uses frontier LLMs, not a small model — [Compositional Neuro-Symbolic Reasoning, arXiv:2604.02434](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02434)
- ARC-AGI-3 (interactive environments) was released in March 2026 with humans at 100% and frontier systems below 1%; ARC Prize 2026 results are scheduled for 4 December 2026 and were not available when these notes were written (the schedule is snippet-level) — [ARC-AGI-3, arXiv:2603.24621](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24621)

### Inferences
- Ingredient ranking by measured effect on ARC: (1) adapting to the test task (5% -> 29%, 18.3% -> 44.5%, "no static transduction above 11%"); (2) rule-preserving augmentation at training time (removing the transformations costs 16 solved tasks on an 80-task subset; RE-ARC pairs 0 -> 1,000 per task: 31.5% -> 54.0%); (3) scoring or voting across augmented views (+10.75 for TRM, +13.9 for VARC, +18 then +5 for the ARChitects); (4) induction plus transduction ensembles (38% and 43% -> 56.75%); (5) an outer refinement loop (+13 points for one extra loop in HRM). Architecture novelty (hierarchy, depth of recursion at inference) ranks last: a same-size Transformer is within 5 points of HRM and TRM's first recursion step already yields 94% of its final score.
- Smaller is often better at this data scale: 2 layers beat 4 (TRM), 18M beats 66M (VARC), and 76K parameters with a description-length objective reach 20% with no training set. A 12.4M-parameter coder is not undersized by ARC standards; the levers are data augmentation and how test-time compute is spent.
- What transfers to a sentence plus one example on a CPU:
  - Product-of-views scoring transfers directly as a selection rule: score each candidate program with the coder under several rule-preserving views of the task (paraphrases of the sentence, renamed variables, reordered arguments, an equivalent re-rendering of the example) and multiply. The ARChitects' numbers show this is a selection gain (+5.1 points) separate from candidate generation.
  - Threshold DFS instead of a fixed beam gives a candidate set whose size adapts to the model's uncertainty, and the ARChitects' recall/selection table is a quantitative warning that pushing recall (76.0% -> 83.5%) by lowering the threshold yields almost no selected-accuracy gain (71.6% -> 71.8%) unless selection improves.
  - Induction-first ensembling transfers as a rule for combining the engine, the coder and (if built) an output-property predictor; agreement between routes is a consistency signal.
  - Hindsight relabeling (CodeIt 42 -> 59; SOAR 14.25% -> 36.25%) transfers as offline self-training and needs no test-time gradient steps.
- What does not transfer as is:
  - Leave-one-out test-time training needs at least two demonstrations of the test task; with exactly one example there is nothing to hold out. The only test-time training signal available is self-generated: programs sampled for the task, relabeled with their own outputs (this is what SOAR's test-time rounds and CodeIt do). CodeIt's "one demonstration" ablation (59 -> 51 solved, policy-only 49 -> 34) is the only direct measurement found of how much is lost with one pair.
  - Puzzle-identity embeddings (HRM, TRM) require training on the test task's own demonstrations and give 0% for an unseen identity; they are transductive memorization plus augmentation and have no analogue for unseen language-described tasks.
  - Per-task gradient adaptation costs 70 s on an H100 (VARC), about 7 minutes on an A100 (Akyürek: 12 h per 100 tasks), 20 minutes on an RTX 4070 (CompressARC); none of this fits a few seconds on 4 CPU cores. The CPU-sized analogue of "adapt to the task" is updating a per-task grammar or operator-weight vector from the search's own near misses (Probe-style), not updating network weights.
  - LPN's latent-space gradient search needs a differentiable decoder that maps a latent to outputs; a coder that emits discrete programs has no gradient path from the example to its tokens. The transferable part is the idea of an amortized guess followed by search initialized from that guess (the encoder initialization was essential: 15.5% versus 10.75% from a random start).
- The CompressARC argument (shortest joint description generalizes) is the same principle as coder-reviewer scoring: -log P(program | sentence, example) - log P(sentence | program) is a two-part code length for the task. It supports ranking by joint description length rather than by program likelihood alone.

### Gaps
- NVARC's model sizes and the exact role of its TRM components were not found in the report or blog; MindsAI's 2025 score is reported inconsistently (12.64% versus 15.42%).
- HRM's 40.3% / 5.0% figures are taken from the comparison tables in the TRM, VARC and CompressARC papers and from the ARC Prize analysis; the HRM abstract page itself gives no number.
- The Cognizant result is a blog post with self-reported public-set numbers and training that included the public evaluation demonstrations; it is not comparable to private-set scores.
- ARC Prize 2026 final results (due 4 December 2026) were not yet published.
- No ARC-line paper studies a setting with a natural-language description of the task; all transfer statements above are inferences.

## 5. Library and abstraction learning

### Takeaway
Learned abstractions are reused on unseen tasks when three conditions hold together: the tasks come from one structured family, abstractions are admitted by a corpus-level compression criterion (they must shorten many programs), and the search or model is then retrained or documented so that it can use them. When abstractions are written per problem by a language model without such a criterion, measured reuse is close to zero and the reported accuracy gains come from other mechanisms.

### Cited Findings
- DreamCoder admits a routine only if it lowers the total description length of the library plus all solved programs after refactoring (a version space of about 10^6 nodes represents about 10^14 refactorings), and retrains its recognition model on replays and samples from the new library; on held-out text-editing tasks the learned library plus recognition model moves the solve rate from 3.7% to 79.6%; library depth correlates with held-out performance at r = 0.79; the list domain ends with "around 20" learned routines — [DreamCoder, arXiv:2006.08381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08381)
- Stitch (Bowers, Olausson, Wong, Grand, Tenenbaum, Ellis, Solar-Lezama; POPL 2023): top-down, branch-and-bound search for the abstraction that maximizes corpus compression; "3-4 orders of magnitude faster and uses 2 orders of magnitude less memory" than DreamCoder's compressor; tens of milliseconds per abstraction on DreamCoder's domains; held-out compression ratios 2.55x-11.57x on eight 250-program datasets (0.24 s to 77 s). It cannot learn higher-order abstractions and matches syntactically (rewrites such as commutativity are only prototyped); the text read reports held-out compression, not downstream synthesis — [Stitch, arXiv:2211.16605](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.16605)
- LILO (Grand, Wong, Bowers, Olausson, Liu, Tenenbaum, Andreas; ICLR 2024): LLM-guided search + Stitch compression + automatic documentation of abstractions. Offline test (enumerative search only, no language, held-out tasks; mean of 3 runs): base DSL 22.00 / 29.13 / 0.90 (REGEX / CLEVR / LOGO); DreamCoder library 41.60 / 91.59 / 30.63; LILO library 64.27 / 92.56 / 41.14 — [LILO, arXiv:2310.19791](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19791)
- LILO online: giving the LLM raw Stitch abstractions with anonymous names (fn_42) hurt relative to the plain LLM solver (REGEX 53.20 vs 76.13; LOGO 21.02 vs 32.13); adding names and docstrings recovered +9.73 on REGEX and +2.27 on CLEVR but nothing on LOGO, where the generated documentation was semantically wrong; the full system reaches 77.07 / 96.76 / 48.95 versus DreamCoder 43.93 / 94.50 / 28.53. One round of LLM synthesis is estimated to cost as much as 48 CPU-hours of DreamCoder search — [LILO, arXiv:2310.19791](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19791)
- ReGAL (Stengel-Eskin, Prasad, Bansal; ICML 2024): an LLM refactors batches of solved programs into helper functions, verifies them by execution, and periodically edits and prunes the code bank; at test time up to 20 helpers are retrieved with demonstrations. CodeLlama-13B: LOGO 45.6 -> 57.1, Date 42.8 -> 68.9, TextCraft 18.8 -> 26.9; but CodeLlama-7B on LOGO 34.5 -> 34.5 and several MATH/TabMWP cells go down (for example CodeLlama-13B TabMWP 29.1 -> 27.9) — [ReGAL, arXiv:2401.16467](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16467)
- ReGAL ablations (dev, LOGO / Date / TextCraft): full 55.0 / 77.0 / 34.12; without pruning the code bank 52.0 / 65.5 / 25.26; without editing 53.3 / 69.6 / 27.35; without the easy-to-hard curriculum 36.3 / 56.6 / 28.78; without retry 48.3 / 51.9 / 30.43 — [ReGAL, arXiv:2401.16467](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16467)
- "Library learning doesn't" (Berlot-Attwell, Rudzicz, Si; 2024): in LEGO-Prover, of about 20,000 learned lemmas exactly one was reused verbatim, once, across 495 solved miniF2F problems, and no lemma was reused more than once even by name; in TroVE the final libraries held 15 functions and there were 3 successful reuses in 3,201 MATH questions — [Library Learning Doesn't, arXiv:2410.20274](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20274)
- Same paper, ablations: TroVE with the reuse mechanism disabled is as good or better (counting 0.250 vs 0.236, number theory 0.290 vs 0.258, both significant); LEGO-Prover without cross-problem sharing solves one problem fewer of 12. The authors attribute the systems' gains to self-correction and to ensembling / self-consistency, and conclude that "accuracy is misleading in isolation" and reuse must be measured directly — [Library Learning Doesn't, arXiv:2410.20274](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20274)
- LAPS: language-guided compression yields libraries that help plain enumeration on held-out tasks (Strings 27.2 -> 33.2, Graphics 41.44 -> 62.16, Scenes 53.6 -> 93.04 relative to DreamCoder's best library), and the authors warn that poor abstractions "increase the combinatorial search space" — [LAPS, arXiv:2106.11053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.11053)
- PATOIS (Shin, Allamanis, Brockschmidt, Polozov; NeurIPS 2019) mines common code idioms from a corpus, adds them to the target language, and trains a tree-based neural synthesizer to emit them, reporting accuracy gains on two semantic parsing datasets (abstract only read; numbers not verified) — [PATOIS, arXiv:1906.10816](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.10816)
- In ARC Prize 2025, one runner-up (Pang) used evolutionary program synthesis in Python "with a dynamically created abstraction library" (no numbers in the report) — [ARC Prize 2025 report, arXiv:2601.10904](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10904)

### Inferences
- Reuse happens under corpus-level compression plus retraining of the consumer: DreamCoder (3.7% -> 79.6%), LAPS and LILO's offline test (22.00 -> 64.27 on REGEX) all pick abstractions because they shorten many training programs, and then either retrain a recognition model or search with a library-derived prior. Reuse fails when abstractions are produced one problem at a time (LEGO-Prover, TroVE), which is the same pattern as operators written by a teacher from individual failures. The expected outcome of that pattern, per the measurements above, is single-use operators plus a larger search space.
- Three practical admission tests follow from the literature: (1) compression: an operator or skeleton is kept only if it shortens at least several distinct held-out right programs; (2) reuse: count how many unseen tasks' right programs use it (the Berlot-Attwell metric), not whether accuracy moved; (3) pruning: periodically remove entries that fail (1) or (2), since ReGAL loses 3 to 11.5 points without pruning and LAPS notes that poor abstractions raise search cost. With about 780 operators, every single-use operator also adds candidates that can match one example by coincidence.
- A consumer has to be taught each abstraction. For an LLM that means names and documentation (LILO); for a from-scratch coder it means retraining on programs rewritten to use the abstraction (DreamCoder's replays and fantasies; PATOIS). Forcing skeleton tokens at search time without such retraining leaves the hole-filling distribution untrained for that context.
- Stitch-style compression is cheap enough (milliseconds to seconds per abstraction on corpora of hundreds of programs) to run on a CPU over the corpus of right programs (engine solutions plus teacher one-liners). A direct offline reach metric is available: the share of held-out teacher solutions whose depth drops to 2 or less in the extended library.

### Gaps
- No study was found of library learning in a setting with one example plus a sentence and a from-scratch model; DreamCoder, LAPS and LILO use 5-15 examples per task or an LLM.
- Stitch's downstream-synthesis effect is not quantified in the text read; PATOIS numbers were not read; ReGAL's helper-reuse counts are only in a figure.
- "Library learning doesn't" examines two LLM systems on mathematics benchmarks; whether its negative finding holds for compression-based systems on structured program domains is not tested there (the DreamCoder, LAPS and LILO offline results suggest it does not).

## 6. Extending the program space beyond one-line expressions, and the trade-off between reach and coincidental matches with one example

### Takeaway
Neural-guided search has been pushed to statements with state (14 statements), loops and conditionals (Karel, restricted C), lambdas and higher-order functions, and recursion, but always inside narrow DSLs and with 2 to 5 informative examples per task. Every measurement that enlarges the space or weakens the evidence shows the share of consistent-but-wrong programs rising, from about 10% (strong language prior plus several tests) to between 60% and over 90% (examples only, a single example, or weak tests), and the theory says this cannot be avoided without ordering the hypothesis space or adding evidence. No source gives a quantitative law for one example; only scattered data points exist.

### Cited Findings

#### Larger program spaces reached by neural-guided search
- Statements with mutable state: PCCoder's state is the set of live variables after executing each statement, with a learned drop of variables; 52-60% of length-14 programs solved after training on lengths up to 12, with 5 examples per task — [PCCoder, arXiv:1809.04682](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04682)
- Loops and conditionals in Karel: the execution-guided model predicts a condition, splits the I/O pairs by its truth value, and synthesizes each branch separately; a while loop is treated as an if with an empty false branch; 92.00% generalization with 5 examples and an ensemble, but only about 45% exact match — [Chen, Liu, Song, ICLR 2019 (OpenReview)](https://openreview.net/forum?id=H1gfOiAqYm)
- Restricted C with for-loops, branches and integer arithmetic (programs up to 256 tokens, constants in [-4, 4], 5 I/O pairs): 55.2% generalization with a learned latent executor versus 37.6% for RobustFill; exact match is "mostly nearly 0", so nearly every success is a different program from the reference — [LaSynth, arXiv:2107.00101](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.00101)
- Lambdas and higher-order functions as the looping mechanism: LambdaBeam (about 13M parameters) solves 67.2 of 100 handwritten list tasks of weight up to 19; its authors state that earlier neural-guided searches could not handle loops, higher-order functions or lambdas — [LambdaBeam, arXiv:2306.02049](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02049)
- Recursion: CrossBeam synthesizes recursive logic programs up to size 19 (for example a 7-clause program with 5 invented predicates) — [CrossBeam, arXiv:2203.10452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10452); DreamCoder solves all 20 basic recursive list tasks starting from 1959 Lisp primitives in roughly five days on 64 CPUs and needs about a CPU-year to rediscover fold/unfold-style recursion schemes — [DreamCoder, arXiv:2006.08381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08381)
- General programs from examples only (PSB2; Helmuth and Kelly; 2021): 25 problems needing loops, conditionals and several data types; each run gets 200 training cases and 60 million program executions; PushGP solves 13 of 25 problems at least once in 100 runs, average success rate 10 of 100. Even with 200 training cases, some training-perfect programs fail the 2,000 unseen cases: generalization rate 0.95-1.0 for most problems, but 0.67 (GCD), 0.74 (Twitter), 0.86 (Middle Character), and 0.00 for Bouncing Balls (2 training solutions, neither generalizes) — [PSB2, arXiv:2106.06086](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06086)
- Whole programs by sampling from large models (AlphaCode; Li et al.; 2022; 284M to 41B parameters): "less than 1% of samples from our models pass example tests"; with up to 10^6 samples, 82-92% of problems have at least one sample that passes the example tests; clustering samples by their outputs on model-generated inputs lifts the 10-submission solve rate from 28.2% to 31.8% (validation, 100k samples) and 27.7% to 29.6% (test) — [AlphaCode, arXiv:2203.07814](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07814)

#### Measurements of consistent-but-wrong programs
- False-positive rate of test suites (a program passes the available tests but is wrong): APPS 60%; HumanEval 30%; raw CodeContests 62%; CodeContests after mutation-based test generation 4% (average tests per problem 12.4 -> 203.7) — [AlphaCode, arXiv:2203.07814](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07814)
- HumanEval with 80x more tests (HumanEval+) lowers pass@k "by up to 19.3-28.9%" across 26 models and changes model rankings — [EvalPlus, arXiv:2305.01210](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01210)
- MBPP with three tests: about 12% of solved problems fail adversarial extra tests — [Austin et al. 2021, arXiv:2108.07732](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.07732). ARC with 2-4 demonstration pairs: about 9% of samples that fit them are wrong on the test input — [BARC, arXiv:2411.02272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02272)
- One example, no language: a random member of the consistent frontier is right 13.7-14.7% of the time — [Ellis and Gulwani 2017, IJCAI](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2017/227). Single user-written examples for tensor tasks: 5 of 18 led to a false positive — [TF-Coder, arXiv:2003.09040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.09040)
- Examples only, several positive and negative strings: AlphaRegex returns an example-consistent regex for 51.8% of tasks but the intended one for 3.6% — [OpSynth, arXiv:2010.01678](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01678)
- Question answering from a single denotation (Pasupat and Liang; ACL 2016): on average about 25,000 logical forms per question execute to the right answer; executing them on 30 "fictitious worlds" (perturbed tables) splits them into an average of 1,237 behavior classes; asking annotators for the answer on at most 5 chosen worlds pruned 92.1% of the spurious logical forms (98.3% with ideal answers; crowd answers wrongly removed the correct class in 9% of examples), and choosing the worlds by expected information gain isolates a single class in 32.7% of questions versus 22.6% for random worlds — [Pasupat and Liang 2016, arXiv:1606.06900](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06900)
- More candidates, same selection: lowering the ARChitects' sampling threshold raises candidates per task from 9.3 to 84.7 and recall from 76.0% to 83.5%, but selected accuracy only from 71.6% to 71.8% — [Product of Experts, arXiv:2505.07859](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.07859). In SOAR the vote over consistent programs is on average 9.5 points below the oracle — [SOAR, arXiv:2507.14172](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14172). For a mid-sized code model, 53 of 164 HumanEval problems had a correct sample that was not ranked first — [CodeT, arXiv:2207.10397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10397)
- Example informativeness: DeepCoder's authors note their five examples with large integers are more informative than typical ones — [DeepCoder, arXiv:1611.01989](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.01989); TF-Coder's false positives occurred only where the output had very few elements, all present in the input — [TF-Coder, arXiv:2003.09040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.09040); on Karel, well-designed examples give 41.16% exact match / 48.52% functional equivalence versus 29.44% / 33.08% for random examples with the same synthesizer, and a learned query network matches the well-designed ones (41.12% / 46.64%) — [Neural program synthesis with query, arXiv:2205.07857](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.07857)

#### Theory and principled mitigations
- "Overfitting in synthesis" (Padhi, Millstein, Nori, Sharma; CAV 2019) defines the potential for overfitting as the number of grammar expressions that match all given examples but not the specification, and proves: (no free lunch) for any budget of m examples a sufficiently expressive grammar contains a problem the learner cannot solve within m examples; (inevitability) such a grammar has a problem with spurious candidates for every set of m examples; (monotonicity) a sub-grammar never has more spurious candidates than the grammar that contains it — [Padhi et al. 2019, arXiv:1905.07457](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07457)
- Padhi et al., evidence on 180 invariant-synthesis problems under six grammars of increasing expressiveness: adding scalar multiplication makes CVC4 fail on 21 problems it solves with a smaller grammar; 65% of successful run pairs are slower with the more expressive grammar, and in 27% of those the number of counterexample rounds rose (overfitting, not just a bigger space); one case goes from 19 rounds to 88 — [Padhi et al. 2019, arXiv:1905.07457](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07457)
- Padhi et al., mitigations: run one learner per grammar in parallel and return the solution from the least expressive grammar that succeeds (PLearn), or interleave the grammars in a single enumerator (hybrid enumeration; overhead 0.97x-1.04x). With hybrid enumeration failures fall instead of rising as the top grammar grows; 117 benchmarks in 429 s versus 115 in 2,191 s for the competition-tuned version of the same tool — [Padhi et al. 2019, arXiv:1905.07457](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07457)
- Occam-style bound: a consistent hypothesis from a class H has error at most epsilon with probability at least 1 - delta when log|H| is at most of order epsilon*m - log(1/delta); the proof bounds the chance that some bad hypothesis is consistent with all m samples by |H|(1 - epsilon)^m — [Occam learning (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam_learning)
- Larger constant sets shrink reach under a fixed budget: adding literals to the DSL lowers solved string tasks from 184 to 132 (Bee Search) and from 180 to 117 (BUSTLE) at 120 minutes per task — [Bee Search, arXiv:2310.04327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04327)
- Solutions built by case splitting generalize worse: CVC4's string solutions average 0.42 if-then-else per example and 68.1% accuracy on unseen inputs versus 0.01 and 100% for Probe's — [Probe, arXiv:2010.08663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08663)
- Resolving ambiguity by generating more examples (Laich, Bielik, Vechev; ICLR 2020): the specification "is often limited, containing as few as one or two input-output examples"; the method iteratively finds inputs on which candidate programs disagree and uses learned models to decide which output is correct, reducing "selecting which program generalizes" to "deciding which output is correct", which can be trained on program outputs alone (abstract only read) — [Laich et al., ICLR 2020](https://iclr.cc/virtual_2020/poster_BJl07ySKvS.html)
- Selecting among plausible programs with unreliable tests (B4; Chen et al.; ASE 2024): the optimal rule is the posterior over observed pass/fail patterns; an approximation improves on the strongest heuristic by up to 50% relative in the hardest settings (abstract only read) — [B4, arXiv:2409.08692](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08692)

### Inferences
- A counting model explains the reported pattern for the target system. Expected spurious matches are roughly (number of distinct behaviors explored) x (probability that a wrong behavior reproduces the one output). The second factor is set by how much information the output carries given the input: tiny for a long list of unusual values, large for a boolean, a small integer, or an output that is a piece of the input (the TF-Coder and DeepCoder observations). Opening every gate, going to depth 3, or adding hole-filling multiplies the first factor by orders of magnitude while the second stays fixed, so the added example-consistent hits are mostly coincidences unless the added region is ordered by a prior that concentrates on right programs. Padhi's monotonicity theorem is the formal version.
- With m = 1 the Occam bound |H|(1 - epsilon)^m gives no protection for any pool with more than a handful of consistent candidates. Protection can only come from (a) the ordering of hypotheses (language-conditioned probability, stage order, description length) and (b) evidence beyond the example: behavior on unlabeled probe inputs, agreement between independent routes, and predicted properties of outputs. Pasupat and Liang show (b) is powerful: a handful of well-chosen extra worlds removes 92% of spurious candidates, and choosing the worlds to split the candidate set is better than random worlds.
- Design rule from hybrid enumeration: search nested spaces in order of expressiveness and stop at the first hit, rather than one large space. For the target system the natural order is gated depth 1, gated depth 2, model-weighted depth 3+, skeletons with holes, free decoding. The rule only works if early stages have low coincidence rates, which can be measured (run each stage against deliberately wrong outputs of the same type and count matches).
- Loops, state and conditionals add a specific hazard on top of size: code that the single example does not exercise (an untaken branch, a loop body run zero or one time) is unconstrained by the example. Karel-style branch synthesis needs examples on both sides of the condition, and case-splitting solutions generalize worst (68.1%). With one example, constructs whose every part is not exercised by the example should be rejected or deferred to the language prior.
- The route by which the field handles multi-statement programs with weak tests is large language-conditioned models plus massive sampling plus clustering (AlphaCode: under 1% of samples pass, 60% false positives with few tests). A 12M-parameter model cannot follow that route. Extending the language before selection is fixed would therefore mostly add coincidences; if it is done, it should enter as the last stage with the strictest acceptance rule.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies, for one example, how the spurious rate scales with the number of programs explored; the numbers above come from different domains and evidence levels and cannot be combined into a curve.
- The quantitative results of Laich et al. (ICLR 2020) could not be retrieved (OpenReview blocked automated access); only the abstract is reported.
- No neural-guided synthesis paper was found that targets loops or state with a single example.

## 7. Concrete recommendations for this system: what to try first, expected gains and risks

### Takeaway
The system's two symptoms have different cures: reach needs guidance at the level of components and executed values (a per-task grammar fitted to the coder's own samples, then value-level scoring and self-training), not wider beams or deeper blind enumeration; coincidences need staged, calibrated acceptance and selection that uses behavior on probe inputs and several views of the task. Selection should be fixed first, because every reach technique in the literature adds candidates and the measured selection ceiling, not the candidate pool, is what currently limits strict accuracy.

### Cited Findings
- A weak model is a useful component prior: per-task PCFG from 10-100 model samples lifts enumeration from 40% to 58% while the model alone solves 6%; 10 samples are nearly as good as 100 on two of three domains — [HySynth, arXiv:2405.15880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15880)
- A cheap value-level guide trained on a CPU gives 101 versus 74 of 127 tasks at 3.4 s; operator-level premise selection gives nothing in the same domain; model inference costs about 20% of run time — [BUSTLE, arXiv:2007.14381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14381); [CrossBeam, arXiv:2203.10452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10452)
- Updating the grammar during search from near-miss programs: 25 versus 9 solved on a larger grammar — [Probe, arXiv:2010.08663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08663). Best-first bottom-up search by unrounded cost solves 184 of 205 string tasks versus 180 for BUSTLE's rounded costs and 143 for Brute with the same cost model, and the authors attribute HeapSearch's poor showing to its lack of observational equivalence — [Bee Search, arXiv:2310.04327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04327)
- Exact best-first search under a language-conditioned model beats beams of similar cost (48.9% vs 45.8%) and its top consistent program is right about 62% of the time versus about 30% for runners-up — [OpSynth, arXiv:2010.01678](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01678)
- On-policy training and subgoal prediction are what generalize in length and composition: about 20x fewer candidates than BUSTLE — [CrossBeam, arXiv:2203.10452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10452); generalization average 42.4% -> 87.0% — [ExeDec, arXiv:2307.13883](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13883); 10% -> 80% when a from-scratch execution-guided model is put inside a search — [Ouellette 2025, arXiv:2507.15877](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15877)
- Self-training on relabeled attempts: 42 -> 59 solved — [CodeIt, arXiv:2402.04858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04858); 14.25% -> 36.25% for a 7B model, with search alone saturating after about 5.2k attempts — [SOAR, arXiv:2507.14172](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14172); about +10 points out of distribution — [Li and Ellis 2024, arXiv:2406.08316](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08316)
- Selection signals with measured value: outputs on unlabeled inputs (13.7% -> 83.5% from a 1000-program frontier with one example; +7 points over a hand-tuned ranker) — [Ellis and Gulwani 2017, IJCAI](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2017/227); execution agreement on one unlabeled input (MBPP 47.3% -> 58.2%) — [MBR-EXEC, arXiv:2204.11454](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.11454); product of scores over augmented views (+5.1 points) — [Product of Experts, arXiv:2505.07859](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.07859); learned verifier over (sentence, program, execution result) with summation over behaviorally identical programs (+4.6 to +10.9) — [LEVER, arXiv:2302.08468](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08468); reviewer score p(sentence | program) (up to +17 absolute over coder-only reranking) — [Coder-Reviewer, arXiv:2211.16490](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.16490)
- Ordering nested grammars and taking the first hit reduces failures as expressiveness grows, at 0.97x-1.04x cost — [Padhi et al. 2019, arXiv:1905.07457](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07457). Unions of routes beat single routes by about 27-30 points on SyGuS — [Li, Parsert, Polgreen 2024, arXiv:2403.03997](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03997); induction first, transduction as fallback: 38% and 43% -> 56.75% — [BARC, arXiv:2411.02272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02272)
- Wider search has steeply diminishing returns: RobustFill 89% -> 92% from beam 100 to 1000 — [RobustFill, arXiv:1703.07469](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07469); baselines needing beams of 65,536-80,000 still lose to guided bottom-up search — [LambdaBeam, arXiv:2306.02049](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02049), [BUSTLE, arXiv:2007.14381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14381); restarts every 6 s help because it is "difficult to recover from exploring the wrong search direction" — [LambdaBeam, arXiv:2306.02049](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02049)
- Per-task gradient adaptation costs 70 s on an H100, about 12 h per 100 tasks on an A100, or about 20 min per puzzle on an RTX 4070 — [VARC, arXiv:2511.14761](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14761); [Test-time training, arXiv:2411.07279](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07279); [ARC Prize 2025 results blog](https://arcprize.org/blog/arc-prize-2025-results-analysis)
- Abstractions written per problem are essentially never reused (1 verbatim reuse among about 20,000 lemmas; 3 reuses in 3,201 questions), and unpruned libraries cost 3-11.5 points — [Library Learning Doesn't, arXiv:2410.20274](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20274); [ReGAL, arXiv:2401.16467](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16467)

### Inferences
All expected gains below are this researcher's estimates, anchored to the cited numbers and to the system's own reported measurements (unguided widening adds 1-3% reach; about 70 example-consistent candidates per task; a teacher solves about 70% of engine failures inside the same library; right programs sit a median 36 nats below the beam's best). They are not measurements and should be replaced by the diagnostics in step 0.

**Step 0. Four offline diagnostics before building anything (hours to two days, no risk).**
1. Recall versus selection, per stage (engine depth 2, skeleton search, beam): under the strict judge, the share of tasks whose candidate pool contains a right program versus the share where it is selected. This is the ARChitects' table for this system and tells how much of the loss is selection.
2. Component recall: for failures with a teacher solution, are all of its operators among the k most frequent operators in K samples from the coder (k = 20, 50, 100)? This decides recommendation 2. HySynth works because recall of components is high even when whole-program accuracy is near zero.
3. Where the 36 nats sit: per-token surprisal of teacher programs under the coder. If the deficit is concentrated in one or two tokens (a rare operator, an unseen parent-child link), a search that allows one or two forced low-probability choices (limited-discrepancy search around the beam) is cheap; if it is spread out, only retraining (recommendation 5) helps.
4. Chance-match rate per stage and output type: run each stage against wrong targets (the output of another task of the same type, or of a random program) and count matches. This yields the coincidence rate that recommendation 1 needs.

**Recommendation 1 (first). Staged search with calibrated acceptance.** Order the hypothesis spaces by expressiveness (gated depth 1 and 2; model-weighted deeper search; skeletons with holes; free decoding), accept a stage's hit only when the measured chance-match rate for that stage and output type is below a threshold, and otherwise keep collecting candidates and decide by recommendation 3; for low-information outputs (booleans, small integers, outputs copied from the input) require agreement between two independent routes or abstain. Basis: hybrid enumeration and the monotonicity theorem (Padhi et al.), TF-Coder's false-positive pattern, induction-first ensembles. Expected: little change in the number of tasks with a consistent program, a large drop in the share of accepted programs that fail the strict judge, and a small direct strict-accuracy gain (0 to +2 points); its main value is that later reach gains stop being diluted by coincidences. Risk: abstentions on tasks that were right by luck; needs the wrong-target calibration to be representative. Cost: low (bookkeeping plus one calibration run).

**Recommendation 2 (first, if diagnostic 2 is favorable). Replace keyword gates by a per-task grammar fitted to the coder's samples, and enumerate in cost order.** Take the programs already produced by the beam (or 32-128 extra samples), count operators and parent-child operator pairs, smooth, turn into costs, and enumerate bottom-up by total cost with observational equivalence (Probe or Bee Search style) instead of by depth; update the weights once or twice from near misses (programs whose output is close to the target by type, length or shared elements) and restart, as Probe does. Basis: HySynth (40% -> 58%), Li et al. (union 80.1%), Probe (25 vs 9), TF-Coder (weights worth 10 of about 62 tasks), Bee Search. Why it can beat "open every gate or go to depth 3": the same budget is spent on perhaps 30-60 likely operators instead of about 780, a reduction of roughly (780/50)^3, about 3,800x, at depth 3, so depth 3-4 becomes affordable within seconds for the weighted subset. Expected: +3 to +8 points of example-consistent reach on tasks the engine now fails, of which perhaps a third to a half survive the strict judge when combined with recommendations 1 and 3 (+1 to +4 strict points). Risks: the coder's samples share its blind spots, so missing operators stay missing (smoothing only partly helps); HySynth's warning that irrelevant operators make guided search slower than unguided; more distinct behaviors explored means more coincidences if recommendation 1 is absent. Cost: moderate (a cost-ordered enumerator over the existing operator library).

**Recommendation 3 (first). Selection by behavior clusters, views and output features.** (a) Group candidates by their outputs on a set of probe inputs and score each group by the summed model probability of its members rather than by its best member (LEVER's marginalization; AlphaCode's clustering). (b) Score each candidate with the coder under several rule-preserving views of the task (paraphrases, renamed variables, reordered arguments) and multiply (product of experts). (c) Add features of the outputs across probe inputs to a small learned ranker: same type and shape as the example output, no exceptions, not constant, not the identity, plus stage index, program cost, coder and reviewer scores (Ellis and Gulwani's output features). (d) Choose probe inputs that split the leading groups, not random ones (Pasupat and Liang: 32.7% vs 22.6% single-class isolation). Expected: +2 to +5 strict points on tasks whose pool contains a right program, discounted from the published +5 to +10 because the model is small, views from one model are correlated, and parts of this (consensus on probe inputs, a reviewer, an execution-reading verifier) may already be in place. Risk: invalid probe inputs produce exceptions that look like agreement; a learned ranker can overfit a small development set.

**Recommendation 4 (second). Value-level guidance and a value head, trained on-policy.** For the engine: a small feed-forward scorer over property signatures of (intermediate value, target output, description features) that reweights costs, as in BUSTLE (trainable on a CPU in hours; about 20% overhead). For the postfix coder: a value head that predicts success from the executed partial state, used to prune or resample partial programs (REPL), and training on the states reached by its own search rather than only on reference prefixes (CrossBeam). Replace the single fixed-width beam by several short diversified searches or restarts across the 4 cores (LambdaBeam's restarts; PCCoder's widening beam). Expected: this is where the literature gets programs of 10-20 nodes; for this system the gain is uncertain because property signatures over one example are binary and Python values are heterogeneous. Estimate +2 to +6 points of reach beyond recommendation 2, with one to two weeks of work. Risk: engineering effort; a value head trained on few positive outcomes.

**Recommendation 5 (second, slow but the largest long-run lever). Self-training on relabeled search attempts with generated descriptions.** Every program executed during searches on training tasks is a correct solution to the task defined by its own output; add such programs, chosen for diversity and closeness to real failures, with a description produced by the reviewer model or by operator templates, and retrain; distill teacher solutions for failures the same way. Basis: CodeIt (42 -> 59), SOAR (2.5x for a 7B model; greedy-diverse selection beats uniform relabeling 36.46 vs 32.38), Li and Ellis (+10 points out of distribution), LAPS (template language transfers to human language), DreamCoder's replays and fantasies. This attacks the 36-nat gap at its source, the coder's distribution. Expected: unknown for a from-scratch 12M model. CodeIt's from-scratch ablation reached 35 solved versus 59 with a pretrained policy, began to generalize between tasks only after about 50 iterations, and stayed below a mutation-only search baseline (42); so a positive but slower and smaller effect is the realistic expectation, and several retraining cycles are needed before judging it. Risks: drift toward synthetic language and synthetic program statistics; generated descriptions that do not resemble real task sentences; each cycle costs a retraining run.

**Recommendation 6 (second). An output-property predictor as a second opinion.** Train a small head to predict properties of the output for a new input from (description, example, input): type, length relation to the input, sortedness, membership, sign, emptiness on edge cases. Use it only where leading behavior groups disagree and only when it is confident. Basis: Laich et al. (decide which output is correct instead of which program), the complementarity of induction and transduction (BARC), 87.5% accuracy for predicting property signatures of compositions (Odena and Sutton). Expected: a modest, targeted selection gain (+1 to +3 strict points); also a fallback answer route. Risk: a 12M model's property predictions are noisy off-distribution.

**Recommendation 7 (hygiene). Prune and grow the library by measured reuse.** Keep an operator or skeleton only if it shortens several distinct held-out right programs; mine new ones by Stitch-style compression over the corpus of right programs rather than from single failures; retrain the coder on programs rewritten to use what is kept. Expected: no direct accuracy jump; fewer candidates per task (less coincidence) and cheaper deep search. Risk: removing rarely used but necessary operators; measure reach on held-out teacher solutions before and after.

**Not recommended now.** (a) Wider beams or more samples: 36 nats is out of reach for any probability-ordered search. (b) Uniform deeper enumeration or opening all gates: already measured at +1-3% and it maximizes coincidences. (c) Per-task gradient test-time training at inference: minutes on a GPU in every published case, and leave-one-out is impossible with one example. (d) HRM/TRM-style recursive transduction: it depends on per-task identity embeddings trained on the test task's own demonstrations and on voting over hundreds of augmentations. (e) Loops, state and multi-statement programs: defer until recommendations 1 and 3 show the selection ceiling has moved; then add as the last, strictest stage.

**Suggested order and what would change it.** Step 0, then recommendations 1 and 3 together (selection), then 2 (reach), then 5 in the background, then 4 and 6. If diagnostic 1 shows that the pool already contains a right program for most failures, skip 2 and put all effort into 3, 6 and 1. If diagnostic 2 shows low component recall, skip 2 and go to 5 and 4. If diagnostic 3 shows the deficit concentrated in single tokens, try limited-discrepancy search around the beam before anything else, since it costs a day.

### Gaps
- None of the cited gains was measured in the target regime (one sentence, one example, about 780 operators, a 12M from-scratch model, 4 CPU cores, a few seconds). The closest regimes are TF-Coder (often one example, 134 operations, minutes), Ellis and Gulwani (one example, ranking only), NGDS (one example, 375 training tasks) and BUSTLE at 3.4 s.
- Which selection techniques the system already uses, and with what measured effect, is known to this researcher only from the task description (re-ranking of 40 engine matches, skeleton search, strict judge with 24 probe inputs) and from task titles visible in this session, which suggest that consensus on probe inputs, a reviewer score P(description | program), a verifier that reads execution results, a ranking loss against example-only-consistent programs, ensembles of separately trained coders, and training on paraphrased descriptions have already been tried. No project file was read, so their measured effects are unknown here; recommendation 3 (a) and (c) may duplicate that work, while product-of-views scoring at inference, probe inputs chosen to split the leading groups, the wrong-target calibration of recommendation 1 and the sample-fitted grammar of recommendation 2 appear to be new for this system.
- No published result was found on fitting a per-task grammar to samples from a small from-scratch model, on product-of-views scoring with paraphrases of a natural-language description, or on hindsight relabeling with generated descriptions for a from-scratch model; these are transfers, not replications.
